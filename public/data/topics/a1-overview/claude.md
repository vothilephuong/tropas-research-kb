# Weather Forecasting for Astronomical Observation: A Comprehensive Literature Review

The prediction of atmospheric conditions for telescope operations has matured from ad hoc meteorological monitoring into a rigorous, multi-parameter forecasting discipline. **Operational optical turbulence forecasting systems now achieve seeing prediction accuracies of 0.08 arcsec RMSE at 1-hour horizons**, comparable to instrumental measurement uncertainty, marking a transformative advance for queue-scheduled and adaptive-optics-dependent observatories. This review surveys the entire landscape of astronomical weather forecasting as of early 2026: foundational definitions, observatory-specific systems worldwide, critical atmospheric parameters, nowcasting methodologies, and the persistent challenges of hyperlocal prediction at remote mountain summits. The field has accelerated since 2018, driven by the demands of extremely large telescope (ELT) programs, the maturation of machine learning techniques, and the operational deployment of hybrid autoregressive-NWP systems at flagship facilities. For tropical and Southeast Asian observatory sites, the literature remains sparse, with only a single peer-reviewed turbulence forecasting study published to date.

## 1. Defining the discipline: site characterization, astronomical forecasting, and observability

### 1.1 Astronomical site characterization

Astronomical site characterization is the comprehensive, multi-parameter assessment of a location's atmosphere and environment to determine its suitability for hosting optical, infrared, or radio telescopes. It fundamentally differs from standard meteorological site assessment by requiring measurement of parameters unique to astronomical applications: optical turbulence profiles (Cn²), astronomical seeing, precipitable water vapor (PWV), isoplanatic angle (θ₀), wavefront coherence time (τ₀), scintillation, sky brightness, and multi-year statistics of photometric night fractions. Standard meteorological services focus on precipitation, temperature, wind, and cloud cover for human, agricultural, or aviation applications. Astronomical site characterization additionally demands vertical turbulence profiling, quantification of optical path degradation through the entire atmosphere, and surface-layer thermal gradient monitoring affecting dome seeing.

The modern methodology was codified through two landmark ELT site-selection campaigns. **Schöck et al. (2009, PASP, 121, 384; arXiv:0904.1183)** described the Thirty Meter Telescope site-testing campaign across five candidate sites using standardized MASS-DIMM turbulence profilers, SODARs, automatic weather stations with sonic anemometers, all-sky cameras, dust counters, and IR radiometers. **Vernin et al. (2011, PASP, 123, 1334)** established standard procedures and instrumentation for the European ELT site characterization across candidate sites including ORM, Paranal-North, Macón, and Aklim. Varela et al. (2014, PASP, 126, 412) extended this to surface meteorology. The heterogeneity of instrumentation across sites remains a challenge; as Hickson (2019, MNRAS, 482, 4941) noted, comparing different sites is often difficult due to differences in instruments and techniques employed.

### 1.2 Astronomical weather forecasting

Astronomical weather forecasting is the specialized prediction of atmospheric parameters that standard weather services do not forecast, including seeing (ε), Cn² profiles, isoplanatic angle (θ₀), coherence time (τ₀), ground-layer fraction (GLF), and fine-scale PWV. The economic imperative is substantial: **ESO charges approximately 83,000 EUR per night of VLT time**, making optimal scheduling directly determinative of scientific return. Adaptive optics instruments require specific turbulence conditions to operate effectively, infrared observations require low PWV, and service-mode observing at major facilities uses queue-based scheduling that matches observation requirements to predicted conditions.

The field was founded by Masciadri, Vernin, and Bougeault (1999, A&AS, 137, 185), who first applied the Meso-NH mesoscale atmospheric model to optical turbulence prediction, creating the Astro-Meso-NH code. This was developed through the MOSE (MOdelling ESO Sites) project, which validated Astro-Meso-NH for VLT and ELT sites (Masciadri, Lascaux & Fini, 2013, MNRAS, 436, 1968; Masciadri et al., 2017, MNRAS, 466, 520). The operational transition was declared in 2017, when Masciadri et al. concluded the system was "ready for an operational application." An independent approach using WRF coupled with the Trinquet-Vernin turbulence parameterization was validated at the Observatorio del Roque de los Muchachos by Giordano et al. (2013, MNRAS, 430, 3102), while Osborn and Sarazin (2018, MNRAS, 480, 1278) demonstrated that ECMWF general circulation model output could directly estimate Cn² profiles, offering a computationally cheaper alternative. The most comprehensive recent review is Masciadri (2025, arXiv:2510.16845), covering the full history and state of optical turbulence forecasting including operational systems and machine learning perspectives.

### 1.3 Observability windows and telescope scheduling

Observability windows are time periods during which atmospheric and environmental conditions are predicted to meet the requirements of specific observations. Weather and atmospheric predictions feed into telescope scheduling through queue-based and dynamic scheduling systems. At ESO's VLT, observing blocks (OBs) are ranked and filtered based on wind, seeing, coherence time, ground-layer fraction, and PWV constraints. A recent VLT/ELT short-term scheduling simulator compared "nowcast" versus "precast" seeing models for OB execution, demonstrating that nowcast matching or exceeded precast performance (arXiv:2407.16049, 2024). The Keck Observatory's AstroQ system (Handley et al., 2025, arXiv:2506.08195) solves the "Traveling Telescope Problem" using a 22-year weather-loss dataset to account for weather uncertainty in completion rate forecasting. Gemini Observatory operates one of the most sophisticated queue systems, with multiple variant observing plans prepared nightly for different condition categories. Lacroix et al. (2025, arXiv:2603.18730) formalized the mathematical optimization of scheduling under uncertain clear-night availability, while the GLORIA robotic telescope network implemented probabilistic algorithms outperforming weather-forecast-based and fuzzy-logic scheduling approaches.

## 2. Observatory forecasting systems by geographic region

### 2.1 Northern Chile: the Atacama corridor

**ESO Paranal Observatory (VLT)** — 24°37′33″S, 70°24′12″W, 2635 m — hosts the most advanced operational forecasting infrastructure in astronomy. The Astronomical Site Monitor (ASM) suite includes a DIMM (operational since 1988), MASS, MASS-DIMM, and a **Stereo-SCIDAR** turbulence profiler commissioned in April 2016 that has acquired over 10,000 full-atmosphere Cn² profiles with 250 m altitude bins (Osborn et al., 2018, MNRAS, 478, 825). The **LHATPRO** (Low Humidity and Temperature PROfiling) 183 GHz radiometer, operational since 2012, measures PWV with accuracy better than 0.1 mm across the 0.5–9 mm range, with 1-second cadence and all-sky scanning capability (Kerber et al., 2012, Proc. SPIE 8446; Kerber et al., 2014, MNRAS, 439, 247). Querel and Kerber (2014, Proc. SPIE 9147) found PWV remarkably homogeneous across the sky, with median variation of only 0.32 mm peak-to-valley.

The **FATE project** (Forecast Automation System for Telescopes), awarded via ESO open competitive tender to INAF-Arcetri with LaMMA (Florence) managing daily operations, represents the current state of the art. FATE uses Astro-Meso-NH initialized from ECMWF HRES forecasts at 00:00 UT, with nested domains down to sub-kilometer resolution, delivering day-ahead forecasts of seeing, θ₀, τ₀, GLF, wind, temperature, humidity, and PWV approximately two hours before sunset. For short-timescale nowcasting (1–2 hours), the **autoregressive (AR) method** combines Meso-NH model outputs with real-time in-situ measurements. Masciadri, Turchi, and Fini (2023, MNRAS, 523, 3487) demonstrated **seeing RMSE of 0.08 arcsec at the 1-hour horizon** — smaller than instrumental uncertainty — with a factor-of-three improvement over standard day-ahead forecasts. The FAST software suite automates the full pipeline (Masciadri et al., 2024, arXiv:2412.05048). FATE's commissioning phase (September–December 2023) achieved 100% reliability, with operational phase beginning June 2024. [Status: **operational**]

ESO additionally maintains a centralized Astroclimatology service (led historically by Marc Sarazin) providing GFS-based PWV forecasts with Kalman filtering for Paranal, La Silla, and Chajnantor, plus ECMWF-based 2–8 day outlooks (Sarazin et al., 2013, ESO Messenger No. 152, pp. 17–21). [Status: **operational**]

Separately, Osborn and Sarazin (2018, MNRAS, 480, 1278) validated an ECMWF-based turbulence forecasting approach against 83 nights of Stereo-SCIDAR data, achieving wind speed correlation of 0.82 and seeing correlation of 0.62. Cuevas, Curé, and Escárate (2024, MNRAS, 529, 2208) combined Cn² models with WRF at high resolution for Paranal, obtaining seeing RMSE of 0.30 arcsec and bias of −0.1 arcsec. [Status: **research/validated**]

**La Silla Observatory** (29°15′S, 70°44′W, 2400 m) uses ESO's centralized forecast service but lacks a dedicated mesoscale forecasting system. Historical SCIDAR campaigns and LOSSAM photometric quality monitoring are operational.

**Cerro Tololo Inter-American Observatory (CTIO)** (30°10′S, 70°48′W, 2200 m), operated by NOIRLab, runs continuous MASS-DIMM monitoring and the novel **RASICAM** thermal IR (10–12 μm) all-sky camera providing automated real-time cloud detection every 90 seconds for DECam operations (Lewis et al., 2010, Proc. SPIE 7735, 77353C). However, CTIO has **no dedicated NWP-based forecasting**, relying on commercial services (Meteoblue, AccuWeather) and GOES satellite imagery.

**Vera C. Rubin Observatory** (30°14′41″S, 70°44′58″W, 2682 m, Cerro Pachón) employs the Feature-Based Scheduler for autonomous real-time observation selection (Delgado & Schumacher, 2014, Proc. SPIE 9149), using real-time monitoring data rather than predictive atmospheric forecasts. Its Environmental Awareness System provides weather data updated every 0.5 seconds. Full survey operations are expected in 2026. [Status: monitoring only; no dedicated forecast system]

**ALMA** (23°01′44″S, 67°45′18″W, ~5000 m, Chajnantor Plateau) faces unique PWV constraints: Bands 9–10 (602–950 GHz) require PWV below approximately 0.5 mm. Median PWV at Chajnantor is approximately 1.0 mm. GFS-based PWV forecasting with Kalman filtering is operational at APEX (Sarazin et al., 2013). Pozo et al. (2016, MNRAS, 459, 419) validated WRF at 1 km resolution, finding PWV errors below 0.5 mm in 65% of cases with the YSU-Noah configuration optimal for operational forecasting. Marín, Ortiz, and Curé (2023, MNRAS, 522, 457) extended WRF to line-of-sight PWV forecasting across the Chajnantor region. A deep learning approach using **LSTM networks** trained on 183 GHz radiometer and meteorological data achieved MAPE of approximately 22% versus approximately 36% for GFS at 12–24 hour horizons (Matus-Bello et al., 2026, A&A; arXiv:2509.09575). [Status: GFS+Kalman **operational**; WRF and LSTM **experimental**]

**ELT / Cerro Armazones** (24°35′21″S, 70°11′30″W, 3064 m) was characterized during ESO's MOSE project simultaneously with Paranal, with Meso-NH domains covering both sites at 100 m resolution. FATE is explicitly designed for future extension to the ELT. First light is planned for the late 2020s.

**GMT / Las Campanas** (29°00′S, 70°42′W, 2516 m) completed extensive site testing (2005–2010) with 17 instruments, finding **median seeing of 0.62–0.67 arcsec**, median PWV of 3.7 mm with 25% of nights below 1 mm, and median nighttime wind speed of 6.3 m/s (Thomas-Osip et al., 2008, 2010, Proc. SPIE 7012, 7733). No dedicated forecasting system has been published.

### 2.2 Mauna Kea, Hawaiʻi: a centralized model

The **Mauna Kea Weather Center (MKWC)**, operated by the University of Hawaiʻi Department of Meteorology since July 1998, is the singular dedicated atmospheric forecasting service for the entire Mauna Kea observatory complex at approximately 4200 m. All individual observatories are consumers of MKWC products rather than operators of independent systems. This centralized model is unique among major observatory sites worldwide. The MKWC has evolved from MM5-based forecasting (1998), through WRF with an embedded Cn²/seeing algorithm calibrated against MASS-DIMM observations (operational since April 2005), to hybrid WRF plus machine learning approaches (Cherubini, Lyman & Businger, 2022, MNRAS, 509, 232). It provides weather forecasts up to 5 days and seeing outlooks for 5 nights, with twice-daily WRF model runs initialized from NCEP GFS.

The foundational "Starcasting" paper by Businger et al. (2002, BAMS, 83, 858) established the MKWC. Cherubini, Businger, and Lyman (2008a,b, JAMC, 47, 1140 and 3033) developed and refined the optical turbulence modeling over Mauna Kea. Lyman, Cherubini, and Businger (2020, MNRAS, 496, 4734) provided a comprehensive analysis of 10 years of MKWC seeing forecasting, with **RMS errors averaging below 0.25 arcsec since 2012**. The 2022 ML extension uses classification and regression algorithms trained on 10+ years of forecast data and MASS-DIMM observations to predict nightly optical turbulent state for the next 5 nights. [Status: **fully operational** since 1999]

**W. M. Keck Observatory** (19°49′35″N, 155°28′27″W, 4145 m) relies on MKWC forecasts, supplementing with on-site environmental sensors, guide camera seeing estimates (median approximately 0.55 arcsec FWHM), and all-sky cameras (CONCAM).

**Gemini North** (19°49′26″N, 155°28′09″W, 4213 m) uses MKWC products for its queue planning tool (QPT), preparing multiple variant observing plans nightly for different condition categories. It shares a meteorological tower with CFHT and accesses MASS-DIMM data from the shared Mauna Kea instrument tower.

**Subaru Telescope** (19°49′32″N, 155°28′34″W, 4163 m) contributed its VPP700 supercomputer for early MKWC MM5 runs (Ogasawara et al., 2002, Proc. SPIE 4844). Its **SkyMonitor** 10 μm infrared all-sky cloud monitor (Takato et al., 2003, Proc. SPIE 4837) detects thermal emission from cirrus across the full sky. Based on MKWC temperature forecasts, Subaru developed a primary mirror pre-conditioning system that sets the mirror within 1°C of nighttime ambient temperature for over 70% of the time.

**CFHT** (19°49′31″N, 155°28′10″W, 4204 m) operates **SkyProbe**, a real-time V-band sky-transparency monitor measuring cloud attenuation within approximately 5% once per minute (Cuillandre et al., 2002, Proc. SPIE 4844). SkyProbe found 76% of nights clear enough to observe and 56% truly photometric (Steinbring, Cuillandre & Magnier, 2009, PASP, 121, 295; arXiv:0811.2448). CFHT also hosts a MASS-DIMM donated by the TMT project.

**TMT site testing** across five candidate sites (2003–2008) found **median DIMM seeing of 0.602 arcsec** at Mauna Kea 13N at 0.5 μm (Schöck et al., 2009, PASP, 121, 384). The comprehensive site-testing series (Schöck et al. 2009; Travouillon et al. 2009; Skidmore et al. 2009; Els et al. 2009; Otárola et al. 2010, PASP, 122, 470) established that all five candidates were excellent sites with no single obvious best choice. Mauna Kea 13N showed the weakest turbulence at 16 km altitude and the largest isoplanatic angle. As of 2025, NSF dropped support for TMT in favor of GMT; the TMT consortium is exploring La Palma.

General Mauna Kea atmospheric characterization shows the site is dominated by the northeastern Pacific subtropical anticyclone producing a persistent **trade wind inversion at approximately 2000 m**, maintaining dry, stable conditions above. The ground layer is confined within an extremely thin layer (≤80 m exponential scaleheight under typical conditions; Chun et al., 2009, MNRAS, 394, 1121). Median free-atmosphere seeing is **0.42 arcsec** (Chun et al., 2009). PWV monitoring at the CSO 225 GHz tipping radiometer has provided continuous opacity data since the 1990s; median PWV is approximately 1.0–1.6 mm.

### 2.3 Canary Islands: Roque de los Muchachos and Teide

**Roque de los Muchachos Observatory (ORM)** (28.76°N, 17.89°W, 2396 m, La Palma) hosts the GTC (10.4 m), WHT, INT, TNG, NOT, MAGIC telescopes, and is protected under Spain's 1992 "Sky Law." The **ForO (Forecasting the Observatories)** system, developed by the IAC Sky Quality Team, has been **operational since 2018**. ForO runs WRF v3.8 four times daily with 72-hour horizons across three nested domains (9, 3, 1 km resolution) centered on ORM and OT. Primary products include PWV forecasts, with extension to Cn² underway.

WRF seeing forecasting at ORM was validated by Giordano et al. (2013, MNRAS, 430, 3102) using the Trinquet-Vernin parameterization across 4500+ hours of simulations, achieving σ_T = 2 K, σ_V = 3.9 m/s for surface meteorological parameters. **PWV forecasting** was extensively validated by Pérez-Jordán, Castro-Almazán, and Muñoz-Tuñón (2018, MNRAS, 477, 5477) against GNSS measurements, demonstrating correlation coefficients of **R = 0.951 at 24 hours and R = 0.904 at 48 hours**. Median PWV at ORM is 3.5 mm from GNSS data, with PWV below 2 mm 27% of the time. Castro-Almazán et al. (2022, Proc. SPIE; HAL:hal-04604975) reported preliminary Cn² forecasting results with seeing RMSE of 0.25 arcsec. The **calima** (Saharan dust) phenomenon affects visibility, extinction, and air quality, with monitoring via the Copernicus Atmosphere Monitoring Service (CAMS) for aerosol alerts. The February 2020 event was the worst in decades, closing airports and reducing visibility to tens of meters.

**Teide Observatory** (28.3°N, 16.5°W, 2390 m, Tenerife) is also covered by ForO. The Izaña Atmospheric Research Centre (IARC/AEMet) at 2373 m has operated as a WMO Global Atmosphere Watch station since 1916, providing comprehensive aerosol, trace gas, radiation, and meteorological monitoring.

### 2.4 South Africa: Sutherland and the Karoo

**SAAO Sutherland / SALT** (32°22′46″S, 20°48′39″E, 1798 m) operates MASS-DIMM (commissioned March 2010) and a SLODAR from Durham University. Catala et al. (2013, MNRAS, 436, 590) characterized the turbulence profile, finding **median overall seeing of 1.32 arcsec** with the free atmosphere above 1 km contributing only 16% (median 0.41 arcsec) and a distinctive strong upper ground layer at 300–500 m contributing approximately 50% of ground-layer turbulence. **SuthWeatherPredict** uses South African Weather Service (SAWS) Eta model data for 0–48 hour forecasts, including specialized sunset temperature forecasting for SALT dome thermal control. [Status: SAWS-based forecasting **operational**; no NWP-based seeing forecasting]

**SKA-Mid** (approximately 30.7°S, 21.4°E, Karoo) and **SKA-Low** (approximately 26.7°S, 116.6°E, Murchison, Western Australia) focus on radio frequency interference management rather than optical atmospheric characterization, with tropospheric effects relevant primarily for calibration.

### 2.5 China: rapid expansion of site characterization

**Lenghu / Saishiteng Mountain** (38.61°N, 93.90°E, 4200 m, Qinghai) emerged as a major discovery in **Deng et al. (2021, Nature, 596, 353)**, reporting 70% photometric nights, **median seeing of 0.75 arcsec** (DIMM, October 2018–December 2020), PWV below 2 mm for 55% of nights, and extraordinarily stable nighttime temperature variation of only 2.4°C. The 2.5 m Wide Field Survey Telescope (WFST "Mozi") became operational in September 2023, with approximately 30 telescopes planned across 9 projects representing approximately ¥2 billion investment. Monitoring employs DIMM, SQM, all-sky camera, and radiosonde campaigns, but no operational NWP-based forecasting system has been published.

**Ali Observatory** (32°19′N, 80°01′E, 5047–5250 m, Tibet) has been extensively modeled by **Qian et al. (2021, MNRAS, 505, 582)** using WRF with the Trinquet-Vernin parameterization at 1 km resolution, yielding yearly medians of **ε₀ = 0.47 arcsec, θ₀ = 4.35 arcsec, τ₀ = 5.52 ms** (2016 data). Surface layer turbulence was characterized by Qian et al. (2022, MNRAS, 510, 5179) using ultrasonic anemometers on a 30 m tower. Ye et al. (2016, MNRAS Letters, 457, L1) used CFSR 31-year reanalysis to establish Ali as "Asia's Atacama." Qian et al. (2024, Proc. SPIE 13097) are developing an operational forecasting system. [Status: **research/experimental**]

**FAST** (25°48′N, 107°21′E, approximately 1000 m, Guizhou), as a radio telescope, focuses on RFI environment and tropospheric/ionospheric effects rather than optical seeing.

### 2.6 India, Thailand, and the tropical frontier

**Indian Astronomical Observatory, Hanle** (32°47′N, 78°58′E, 4500 m, Ladakh) provides 255 spectroscopic nights per year with annual precipitation below 10 cm. DIMM seeing monitoring commenced January 2023, but seeing values of 0.9–1.2 arcsec are higher than competing sites. No NWP-based forecasting system has been published.

**Devasthal Observatory** (29.36°N, 79.68°E, 2424 m, Uttarakhand) hosts India's largest optical telescope (3.6 m DOT). Sagar et al. (2000, A&AS, 144, 349) measured median seeing of approximately 1.1 arcsec from 80 nights of DIMM data.

⚠️ **Thai National Observatory (TNO) / Doi Inthanon** (18.57°N, 98.48°E, 2457 m, Thailand), operated by NARIT, is the only tropical observatory with a published peer-reviewed atmospheric turbulence forecasting study. **Bran et al. (2024, MNRAS, 530, 1414)** applied **WRF-Chem v4.3.1 coupled with the Trinquet-Vernin seeing model** — the first application of a chemistry-coupled model to astronomical seeing at a tropical site. The model used the MYNN Level 3 PBL scheme and included chemical and aerosol processes with updated anthropogenic emission inventories for northern Thailand, validated against DIMM observations and SLODAR turbulence profiles from December 2022, February 2023, and April 2023 campaigns. The inclusion of biomass burning aerosol effects is novel and highly relevant for Southeast Asian sites affected by seasonal haze. This paper is foundational for any tropical observatory site development. [Status: **experimental/research** — published 2024]

No significant astronomical atmospheric characterization or forecasting studies were identified for observatories in Vietnam, Indonesia, or the Philippines. The Indonesian Bosscha Observatory (Lembang, Java) is operational but lacks modern site characterization studies. Hidayat et al. (2012, MNRAS, 427, 1903) relied entirely on 15 years of satellite data for Indonesian site selection. **This represents a major gap in the literature for tropical/equatorial sites.**

### 2.7 Arizona: the ALTA Center prototype

The **ALTA Center** (Advanced LBT Turbulence and Atmosphere) at the Large Binocular Telescope (Mt. Graham, 32.70°N, 109.89°W, 3221 m) has been **operational since 2016** and is historically significant as the **first automated operational optical turbulence forecast system** for a top-class astronomical telescope. It uses Astro-Meso-NH initialized from ECMWF data, with the AR filter implemented since 2019 (Masciadri, Martelloni & Turchi, 2020, MNRAS, 492, 140). ALTA served as the direct technology prototype for FATE at Paranal.

## 3. Critical atmospheric parameters: measurement, physics, and forecasting

### 3.1 Seeing, Fried parameter, and integrated turbulence quantities

The Fried parameter r₀ is the diameter of a circular area over which the RMS wavefront aberration due to atmospheric turbulence equals 1 radian. It relates to the turbulence integral as r₀⁻⁵ᐟ³ = 0.423(2π/λ)²∫Cn²(h)dh, yielding **seeing ε = 0.98λ/r₀** (conventionally at λ = 500 nm). Typical r₀ values at good sites are 10–30 cm, corresponding to seeing of 0.4–1.0 arcsec. The **isoplanatic angle** θ₀ = 0.314(cos γ)r₀/h̄ defines the angular patch over which a single AO correction is valid, where h̄ is the Cn²-weighted mean altitude. The **coherence time** τ₀ = 0.314r₀/V̄ sets the required AO loop speed. Good-site values are typically θ₀ ≈ 1.5–2.5 arcsec and τ₀ ≈ 3–5 ms (Roddier, 1981, Progress in Optics, 19, 281).

The standard seeing monitor is the **DIMM** (Differential Image Motion Monitor), using two circular sub-apertures to measure differential image motion that cancels telescope tracking errors (Sarazin & Roddier, 1990, A&A, 227, 294). The **MASS** (Multi-Aperture Scintillation Sensor) analyzes stellar scintillation in four concentric annular apertures, with altitude discrimination via Fresnel radius √(λh), fitting scintillation indices to a 6-layer model covering 0.5–16 km (Kornilov et al., 2003; Tokovinin et al., 2007, MNRAS, 381, 1179). Combined MASS-DIMM instruments (Kornilov et al., 2007, MNRAS, 382, 1268) provide simultaneous total seeing and free-atmosphere profiling, enabling real-time θ₀ and τ₀ estimation.

For higher-resolution turbulence profiling, **Stereo-SCIDAR** cross-correlates scintillation patterns from binary stars imaged on separate detectors, covering 0–25 km with approximately 250 m resolution (Shepherd et al., 2014, MNRAS, 437, 3568; Osborn et al., 2018, MNRAS, 478, 825). **SLODAR** (SLOpe Detection And Ranging) cross-correlates Shack-Hartmann wavefront sensor slopes from binary stars for surface-layer profiling up to approximately 1.5 km (Wilson, 2002, MNRAS, 337, 103). **Balloon microthermometers** provide direct in-situ Cn² with approximately 10 m vertical resolution from surface to above 25 km but are non-reusable and limited to campaign-style measurements.

### 3.2 Cloud cover and sky transparency

Cloud detection at observatories employs all-sky cameras in both visible and thermal infrared bands. CTIO's **RASICAM** operates at 10–12 μm to detect thin high cirrus at 8–10 km that visible cameras miss (Lewis et al., 2010, Proc. SPIE 7735). Subaru's **SkyMonitor** uses a 10 μm imager with gold-coated aspheric mirrors for full-sky thermal cloud monitoring (Takato et al., 2003, Proc. SPIE 4837). CFHT's **SkyProbe** provides absolute V-band extinction measurements within approximately 5% per minute (Cuillandre et al., 2002). Machine learning cloud detection from all-sky cameras has advanced rapidly: Mommert (2020, AJ, 159, 178; arXiv:2003.11109) compared ResNet deep learning (85% accuracy) with LightGBM gradient-boosted trees (comparable accuracy, lower cost) using Lowell Observatory data. Li et al. (2022, AMT, 15, 3629) achieved **97.28% accuracy** for TMT-criteria sky classification using random forest features (cloud weight, area ratio, dispersion) from Xinjiang site images. EfficientNet-based deep learning has been applied for real-time precipitation cloud safety warnings at the Iranian National Observatory (2025, 99% rainfall potential accuracy).

Satellite imagery provides crucial context: GOES for the Americas, Meteosat for Atlantic/Africa, and Himawari-8 for the Pacific region. NWP cloud fraction forecasts from ECMWF and GFS remain coarse but provide multi-day outlooks.

### 3.3 Precipitable water vapor

PWV is the vertically integrated atmospheric water vapor column, critical for infrared and submillimeter astronomy because water vapor absorption bands severely attenuate signals across the 1–350 μm and millimeter-wave spectrum. **ALMA's highest frequency bands require PWV below 0.5 mm**, while near-infrared observations are significantly affected above approximately 3–5 mm.

**GPS/GNSS zenith delay** provides continuous all-weather PWV from the separation of zenith wet delay (ZWD) from total delay using Saastamoinen hydrostatic modeling, achieving 1–2 mm RMS accuracy versus radiosondes. **LHATPRO 183 GHz radiometers** at Paranal achieve PWV accuracy better than 0.1 mm with 30 μm precision and 1-second cadence (Kerber et al., 2012, 2014). **225 GHz tipping radiometers** at submillimeter sites (CSO, APEX) measure opacity as a PWV proxy. PWV forecasting spans NWP approaches (GFS with Kalman filtering operational at ALMA; WRF validated at Chajnantor with errors below 0.5 mm in 65% of cases per Pozo et al. 2016; WRF at ORM with R = 0.951 at 24 hours per Pérez-Jordán et al. 2018) to the AR method achieving **gain factors of approximately 8× over standard forecasts** with RMSE of 0.04 mm for PWV ≤ 1 mm (Turchi et al., 2020, MNRAS, 497, 4910), and deep learning LSTM networks reducing RMSE by approximately 50% versus GFS at Chajnantor (Matus-Bello et al., 2026, A&A).

### 3.4 Sky brightness

Natural sky brightness comprises airglow (chemiluminescence from approximately 90 km altitude, contributing approximately 70% of zenith brightness, variable with solar cycle by approximately 0.4 mag), zodiacal light (approximately 0.4 mag brighter on the ecliptic than at the poles), scattered starlight, and diffuse galactic light. Natural dark-sky values reach V ≈ 21.9 mag/arcsec² at La Palma (Benn & Ellison, 2007, La Palma Technical Note 115). The **SQM** (Sky Quality Meter, Unihedron) uses a photodiode with approximately Johnson V-band response and 20° field of view, measuring down to approximately 22.5 mag/arcsec². **ASTMON** (IAC) provides all-sky CCD maps in UBVRI for separating sky brightness, extinction, and cloud contributions. Falchi et al. (2016, Science Advances, 2, e1600377) produced the definitive global atlas of artificial night sky brightness. Sky brightness forecasting remains limited, though Moon illumination and proximity effects are well-modeled analytically.

### 3.5 Wind, temperature, humidity, and dome effects

Wind speed determines dome seeing (thermal boundary layer turbulence inside the enclosure), wind buffeting (structural tracking errors), and surface-layer mechanical turbulence. A **primary mirror 1°C warmer than ambient** degrades seeing to approximately 1 arcsec through convective boundary-layer effects on the mirror surface. Modern telescopes mitigate this through active ventilation, mirror pre-conditioning to predicted nighttime temperature (requiring accurate temperature forecasts from MKWC or similar), thin meniscus mirrors, and optimized dome ventilation. The trade-off between dome flushing (reducing thermal seeing) and wind buffeting (increasing tracking errors) has been studied extensively via CFD modeling for TMT and ELT (Vogiatzis & Angeli, 2006, Proc. SPIE 6271). Temperature inversions at mountain sites are critical: the persistent **trade wind inversion at approximately 2000 m** at Mauna Kea maintains dry, stable free-atmosphere conditions above, while condensation risk monitoring via continuous dew point tracking protects optics.

### 3.6 Boundary layer height and jet stream position

The **atmospheric boundary layer** at mountain observatories is often very shallow at night (tens of meters), with the observatory protruding above the inversion into the free atmosphere. This is measured via aerosol backscatter LIDAR (vertical resolution 3.75–30 m but with a blind zone near the surface), SODAR (effective from approximately 50 to 1000 m, excellent for shallow nocturnal boundary layers), and radiosondes. The ground layer (lowest 200–500 m) typically contains **40–80% of total optical turbulence** at most sites.

The **jet stream position at 200 hPa** is a widely-used proxy for free-atmosphere seeing: wind shear at jet stream altitude generates Kelvin-Helmholtz instability producing optical turbulence layers. Sarazin and Tokovinin (2002, ASP Conf. 266) proposed the effective wind speed V₀ = max(V_ground, 0.4 × V₂₀₀). García-Lorenzo, Fuensalida, and Muñoz-Tuñón (2005, MNRAS, 356, 849) showed the 0.4 constant varies by site. At most mid-latitude sites, the jet stream is strongest in winter, producing worse free-atmosphere seeing.

## 4. Nowcasting methodologies: from persistence to hybrid intelligence

### 4.1 The autoregressive method dominates short-timescale forecasting

The **autoregressive (AR) method** developed by the Masciadri group represents the current state of the art for 1–2 hour nowcasting of optical turbulence parameters. It combines day-ahead Astro-Meso-NH model forecasts with real-time in-situ measurements (DIMM, MASS-DIMM, Stereo-SCIDAR) through autoregressive coefficients computed over a buffer of past nights. First introduced at the LBT (Masciadri, Martelloni & Turchi, 2020, MNRAS, 492, 140) with **seeing RMSE of 0.10 arcsec** at 1 hour and POD of 99% for seeing below the first climatological tertile, the method was extended to all four main astroclimatic parameters at the VLT (Masciadri, Turchi & Fini, 2023, MNRAS, 523, 3487) achieving **RMSE of 0.08 arcsec** — a factor-of-three improvement over standard day-ahead forecasts and a factor-of-two gain over persistence. For PWV, the AR method achieved a **gain factor of approximately 8× over standard forecasts** (Turchi et al., 2020, MNRAS, 497, 4910). Critically, Turchi, Masciadri, and Fini (2022, Proc. SPIE 12185; arXiv:2210.11236) compared the AR method directly with pure ML techniques and found **ML accuracy was not competitive with the AR method or even persistence** for short-term forecasting at the VLT. This finding has significant implications: the physics-informed hybrid approach currently outperforms data-driven methods alone at operational observatories with rich monitoring infrastructure.

### 4.2 Machine learning approaches show promise for longer horizons and sparser sites

Despite the AR method's superiority for short-term nowcasting at well-instrumented sites, ML approaches have demonstrated value across different contexts. **Ni et al. (2022, RAA, 22, 125003)** provided the most comprehensive comparison using LAMOST monitoring data: ARIMA, Prophet (statistical); MLP, XGBoost (ML); LSTM, GRU, Transformer (deep learning). The **Transformer achieved best single-model performance (RMSE 0.0998 arcsec)** while XGBoost offered very competitive accuracy with training times of seconds versus approximately 500 seconds for the Transformer. Feature-level hybrid models combining ML outputs as features for deep learning models outperformed result-level ensembles.

Cherubini, Lyman, and Businger (2022, MNRAS, 509, 232) applied ML to automate the MKWC forecaster's ability to anticipate optical turbulence over 5-night horizons, using GFS model variables as predictors. Sheridan et al. (2025, RNAAS, 9, 25) demonstrated random forest seeing estimation at ORM from only 6 months of weather station data, with maximum wind speed (importance 0.23), air pressure (0.18), and wind speed standard deviation (0.17) as the most important predictors. Shikhovtsev et al. (2024, Atmosphere, 15, 38) used deep neural networks trained on 13-year Maidanak Observatory data with ERA5 inputs, achieving Pearson correlations of 0.48–0.68. At Dome A, Antarctica, where operating a DIMM is extremely challenging, an ML framework using only automated weather station data achieved **RMSE of 0.12 arcsec for 20-minute seeing prediction** (2023, Astronomy and Computing, 43, 100710), representing a 37% improvement over persistence. The pioneering ML turbulence nowcasting study for a major observatory was Milli et al. (2019, Proc. SPIE 11448; arXiv:1910.13767), who coined "turbulence nowcasting" terminology and showed ML's most promise for predicting regime changes rather than incremental variations.

### 4.3 NWP models: WRF and Meso-NH as workhorses

The two dominant mesoscale NWP models for astronomical applications are **Meso-NH** and **WRF**, both typically configured with multiple nested domains reaching sub-kilometer horizontal resolution. Meso-NH, developed at Météo-France, uses an anelastic formulation with a 1.5-order TKE turbulence closure (Cuxart et al., 2000), Bougeault-Lacarrère mixing length, and the ISBA surface scheme. The Astro-Meso-NH extension computes Cn² profiles from the prognostic TKE equation. Configurations typically use 3–5 nested domains from approximately 100 km down to 0.1–0.5 km, initialized from ECMWF HRES at approximately 9 km resolution with 137 vertical levels.

WRF with the **Trinquet-Vernin parameterization** (2007, Environ. Fluid Mech., 7, 397) has been validated at ORM (Giordano et al., 2013), Chajnantor (Pozo et al., 2016), Ali Observatory (Qian et al., 2021, MNRAS, 505, 582), and the Thai National Observatory (Bran et al., 2024, MNRAS, 530, 1414). The Trinquet-Vernin model is a statistical regression approach estimating Cn² from macroscopic meteorological parameters (temperature gradient, wind shear, stability) using empirically derived coefficients, offering simplicity and computational speed compared to the physically-based TKE approach of Masciadri. Cuevas et al. (2024, MNRAS, 529, 2208) found that combining TKE-based models for the boundary layer with different models for the free atmosphere, followed by site-specific calibration, yielded optimal results.

ECMWF IFS and GFS serve primarily as initialization data for mesoscale models and for direct large-scale forecasting. ERA5 reanalysis (0.25° resolution) has become the standard training dataset for ML-based approaches. The HARMONIE-AROME model is used by AEMet for general forecasting in the Canary Islands but has not been specifically validated for astronomical turbulence applications.

### 4.4 Foundation weather models: an unexploited opportunity

Large-scale ML weather foundation models — **Pangu-Weather** (Bi et al., 2023, Nature, 619, 533), **GraphCast** (Lam et al., 2023, Science, 382, 1416), **GenCast** (Price et al., 2024, Nature), and **FourCastNet** (Pathak et al., 2022, arXiv:2202.11214) — operate at approximately 0.25° global resolution trained on ERA5. **No papers were found applying these models to observatory forecasting.** Their resolution is insufficient without downscaling, they lack sub-grid parameterizations for optical turbulence, but their extremely fast inference (10-day forecasts in under 1 minute) and GenCast's probabilistic framework suggest a promising hybrid: foundation models for large-scale pattern detection, mesoscale downscaling for local dynamics, and AR filtering with local measurements. This represents a significant research opportunity.

### 4.5 Sensor fusion and real-time systems remain underdeveloped

Despite the availability of multiple simultaneous data streams at major observatories (DIMM, MASS-DIMM, Stereo-SCIDAR, LHATPRO, all-sky cameras, weather stations, satellite imagery), **no published system integrates all streams in a unified ML or data fusion framework for nowcasting.** The AR method implicitly performs a limited form of sensor fusion by combining model forecasts with selected in-situ measurements. Cloud motion prediction from all-sky camera sequences for sub-hour telescope pointing optimization represents a specific gap: cloud detection has been addressed, but forecasting cloud trajectories for observatory scheduling has not.

## 5. Why hyperlocal prediction remains the hardest problem in astronomical meteorology

### 5.1 Standard models fundamentally misrepresent mountain summits

Global NWP models smooth topography to their grid resolution: at ECMWF's approximately 9 km, and GFS's approximately 13–25 km resolution, mountain peaks are effectively absent. The Andes are largely unresolved in coarse-grid reanalyses. This causes systematic errors in wind flow patterns, temperature inversions, and moisture transport around summits. As Masciadri et al. (2013, MNRAS, 436, 1968) established, "GCMs are not reliable in the low part of the atmosphere, particularly in the proximity of mountain regions, because they have a horizontal resolution that is too low and an orography that is too smooth." Osborn and Sarazin (2018) showed that even at 0.3° ECMWF resolution, wind direction RMSE at Paranal was 29° against Stereo-SCIDAR measurements, attributed to unresolved wind shear. GCMs also lack the temporal sampling needed, providing typically 6-hourly output versus the 2-minute sampling of mesoscale models.

### 5.2 The last-mile downscaling problem

Dynamical downscaling with nested mesoscale models is the dominant solution. The Meso-NH approach uses ECMWF initialization through 3–5 nested domains down to 100–500 m resolution. WRF implementations typically use triple nesting (27/9/3 km or 9/3/1 km). However, Smedstad and Foss (2017, Tellus) found the largest improvement occurs between 9 km and 3 km resolution, with only minor additional gains below 1 km for standard meteorological parameters — though optical turbulence, being sensitive to fine-scale shear and thermal gradients, may benefit more from higher resolution.

Statistical downscaling and ML bypass the computational cost of nested simulations. Cherubini et al. (2022) translated MKWC experience into ML seeing forecasts using GFS variables directly. Sheridan et al. (2025) demonstrated that random forests trained on only local weather station data can provide useful seeing estimates. The AR method represents the most effective hybrid: mesoscale physics provides the baseline state, local measurements correct the residual. No published studies were found on transfer learning between observatory sites, which could enable rapid deployment of ML models at newly instrumented locations.

### 5.3 Data scarcity and validation at remote summits

Mountain summits typically host a single weather station with no nearby radiosonde network. At Paranal, radiosondes are launched from Antofagasta, approximately 120 km away. DIMM instruments require clear skies, bright stars, and careful maintenance. At Dome A, Antarctica, extreme cold, icing of optical elements, and fully unattended operation make continuous DIMM measurements exceptionally challenging. The MOSE project specifically addressed the difficulty of obtaining independent calibration and validation samples (Masciadri et al., 2013), using Generalized SCIDAR as reference after demonstrating MASS had systematic errors of −32% to −65%. Multiple metrics are needed for proper validation: bias, RMSE, standard deviation, probability of detection in tertile bins, and contingency tables. Simple RMSE can mask the ability to detect extreme conditions. Temporal matching through 1-hour moving averages applied to both forecasts and measurements is standard practice.

### 5.4 Tropical observatories face distinct and severe challenges

Tropical sites confront fundamentally different atmospheric dynamics. Indian sites like Devasthal lose approximately three months per year to monsoon (Ningombam et al., MNRAS, approximately 2021). Maritime/archipelagic settings in Southeast Asia produce diurnal convection cycles that are poorly captured by mesoscale models tuned for mid-latitude mountain sites. Hidayat et al. (2012, MNRAS, 427, 1903) found Indonesian cloud cover heavily modulated by ENSO — during El Niño years, clear fractions in eastern Indonesia reached approximately 70% but declined during La Niña. Inherently high atmospheric moisture at tropical sites makes PWV monitoring critical across the near-IR and submillimeter bands. **The WRF-Chem study by Bran et al. (2024) at the Thai National Observatory is the only published optical turbulence forecasting work for a tropical site**, introducing chemistry-coupled modeling to account for biomass burning aerosol effects on seeing — a phenomenon essentially absent at mid-latitude sites. No WRF, Meso-NH, or ML-based seeing forecasting studies exist for other Southeast Asian or Pacific observatory sites, representing the single largest geographic gap in this field.

### 5.5 Model skill versus climatological baselines

Published comparisons consistently show that mesoscale models and hybrid methods outperform climatological baselines. The AR method at VLT achieves POD of 99% for 1-hour seeing forecasts below the first climatological tertile, with a factor-of-three improvement over standard forecasts (Masciadri et al., 2023). Giordano et al. (2013) showed positive seeing forecast skill over climatology at ORM. ML at Dome A demonstrated 37% improvement over persistence (Hou et al., 2023). However, for sites in development lacking continuous monitoring infrastructure, **site selection still relies on satellite/reanalysis climatology for initial screening** (Hidayat et al., 2012; Hickson, 2019), followed by in-situ campaigns of 1–3 years — periods too short to account for multi-decadal climate variability (Haslebacher et al., 2022, A&A, 665, A178; arXiv:2208.04918). Climate change itself threatens long-term site quality: Haslebacher et al. projected approximately 0.2°C per decade temperature increases at eight major observatories, while Cantalloube et al. (2020) found a positive trend in seeing at Paranal from 1986–2020. Seidel, Otárola, and Théron (2023, Atmosphere, 14, 1511) documented ENSO correlation with observing conditions at northern Chilean sites.

## Conclusion: converging frontiers and open problems

This review reveals a field in rapid transition from monitoring-centric operations to predictive, forecast-driven telescope scheduling. Three findings stand out as particularly significant for future research.

First, the **autoregressive hybrid method** combining mesoscale NWP with real-time measurements has proven decisively superior to both pure NWP and pure ML approaches for short-timescale optical turbulence nowcasting, achieving instrumental-accuracy-level predictions at the VLT. The FATE system's operational deployment at Paranal in 2024 establishes a template for ELT-era forecast infrastructure. Extension of this methodology to other sites — including the ELT, GMT, and TMT wherever it is built — is the natural next step.

Second, the **geographic asymmetry** in forecasting capability is stark. Chilean and Hawaiian sites benefit from decades of development and operational systems, Canary Islands sites have validated WRF-based tools, but Southern Hemisphere sites (South Africa, Australia), Asian sites (China, India), and especially **tropical sites have no operational optical turbulence forecasting systems**. The Bran et al. (2024) WRF-Chem study at Thailand's Doi Inthanon is foundational but isolated. For any future tropical observatory development, chemistry-coupled modeling to account for biomass burning, convective variability, and monsoon dynamics will be essential.

Third, large-scale ML weather foundation models (GraphCast, GenCast, Pangu-Weather) remain entirely untapped for astronomical forecasting. Their approximately 25 km resolution is insufficient alone, but their potential as fast, accurate boundary conditions for mesoscale downscaling — replacing traditional GFS/ECMWF initialization — combined with probabilistic frameworks for scheduling under uncertainty, represents the most promising unexplored frontier. The field also lacks ensemble NWP systems specifically designed for optical turbulence, transfer learning between observatory sites, and unified sensor fusion frameworks integrating all available data streams for real-time decision support. Addressing these gaps will define the next generation of astronomical weather forecasting.
