# Intelligent forecasting systems for astronomical observatory operations: a literature review

Observatory operations increasingly depend on atmospheric prediction—from mesoscale numerical weather models achieving **0.08 arcsec seeing RMSE** at the VLT to low-cost Arduino-based cloud sensors protecting university telescopes. This review surveys the full landscape of intelligent forecasting systems across five domains: ESO's integrated monitoring and forecasting infrastructure, the Rubin Observatory's Feature-Based Scheduler, ELT/TMT site-selection campaigns, robotic and small-telescope automation, and Sky Quality Meter networks. The field has evolved from purely reactive threshold-based decision-making toward hybrid systems combining numerical weather prediction with real-time sensor data and machine learning, though purely data-driven ML approaches have not yet matched physics-informed hybrid methods for turbulence forecasting.

## 1. ESO's atmospheric monitoring and forecasting ecosystem

### The Astronomical Site Monitor at Paranal

The ESO Astronomical Site Monitor (ASM) represents the most comprehensive atmospheric monitoring suite at any ground-based observatory. Operational since the late 1990s and substantially upgraded in April 2016, the ASM integrates multiple instruments measuring the full range of parameters relevant to adaptive optics and service-mode scheduling. The **DIMM** (Differential Image Motion Monitor) measures integrated atmospheric seeing at 500 nm, deployed on a 7 m tower 100 m north of UT4. The foundational DIMM technique was established by Sarazin & Roddier (1990, *A&A*, 227, 294–300), and the upgraded DIMM2016 uses a Dall-Kirkham design by Orion Optics. The **MASS** (Multi-Aperture Scintillation Sensor) provides low-resolution Cn² vertical profiles at six layers from 0.5 to 16 km, yielding free-atmosphere seeing, coherence time τ₀, and isoplanatic angle θ₀. It was developed jointly at the Sternberg Institute and CTIO (Kornilov et al. 2003, *Proc. SPIE*, 4839; Tokovinin & Kornilov 2007, *MNRAS*, 381, 1179–1189). Since March 2011, the **SLODAR** (SLOpe Detection And Ranging) profiler, developed by Durham University and ESO, has operated robotically to characterize the ground-layer Cn² profile in the first ~1 km with 50–100 m vertical resolution (Wilson 2002, *MNRAS*, 337, 103–108; Wilson, Butterley & Sarazin 2009, *MNRAS*, 399, 2129–2138).

Higher-resolution turbulence profiling is provided by a **Stereo-SCIDAR** instrument installed at the AT E0 pad, delivering Cn² and wind velocity profiles up to ~20 km with ~200–300 m resolution (Shepherd et al. 2014, *MNRAS*, 437, 3568–3577; Osborn et al. 2018, *MNRAS*, 478, 825–840). Precipitable water vapor (PWV) is monitored continuously by the **LHATPRO** radiometer operating at 183 GHz H₂O and 51–58 GHz O₂ channels, manufactured by Radiometer Physics GmbH. Vaisala weather stations at 2, 10, 20, and 30 m heights on a dedicated tower record wind speed and direction, temperature, humidity, and barometric pressure as one-minute averages. The ALPACA all-sky camera monitors sky transparency. All data are archived in the **ESO Ambient Conditions Database** accessible via archive.eso.org, with historical records extending back to August 1998. The 2016 upgrade is documented by Chiozzi et al. (2016, *Proc. SPIE*, 9913, 991314), and the integrated Paranal-Armazones monitoring plan by Otarola et al. (2024, *Proc. SPIE*, hal-04605058).

### Numerical weather prediction models developed for ESO sites

Three classes of NWP approaches have been validated at ESO sites, representing a hierarchy from global circulation models to sub-kilometric mesoscale simulations.

The most extensively developed system is the **Astro-Meso-NH** code, an extension of the Meso-NH non-hydrostatic mesoscale model (developed by CNRM/Météo-France and Laboratoire d'Aérologie) to compute three-dimensional Cn² maps and derived astroclimatic parameters. The method was pioneered by Masciadri, Vernin & Bougeault (1999a, 1999b, *A&AS*, 137, 185–202 and 203–216) and brought to operational maturity through the **MOSE (MOdelling ESO Sites)** project, led by Elena Masciadri at INAF-Arcetri and co-funded by ESO. The Astro-Meso-NH model uses ECMWF HRES analyses as initial and boundary conditions, employs grid nesting with up to five embedded domains reaching **100–500 m horizontal resolution**, and uses a 500 m-mesh digital elevation model over a 120×120 km domain around Paranal. The MOSE project validated this approach over 129 nights at Paranal and Armazones, achieving **surface temperature RMSE < 1°C**, **wind speed RMSE ~2 m/s**, and **wind direction RMSE ~37°** (Masciadri, Lascaux & Fini 2013, *MNRAS*, 436, 1968–1985; Lascaux, Masciadri & Fini 2013, *MNRAS*, 436, 3147–3166; Lascaux, Masciadri & Fini 2015, *MNRAS*, 449, 1664–1678). Full optical turbulence forecasting (seeing, θ₀, τ₀) was validated against DIMM, MASS, and Generalized SCIDAR using a 53-night primary sample (Masciadri et al. 2017, *MNRAS*, 466, 520–539).

At coarser resolution, Osborn & Sarazin (2018, *MNRAS*, 480, 1278–1299) demonstrated that **ECMWF ERA5 reanalysis** data at 0.3° resolution and 137 model levels can produce useful turbulence forecasts at Paranal, achieving **median Cn² profile correlation of 0.98** with Stereo-SCIDAR. This general circulation model approach processes in seconds rather than hours, though it cannot reproduce extreme events due to limited altitude resolution.

**WRF** (Weather Research and Forecasting) model applications at Paranal have been pursued by Cuevas et al. (2024, *MNRAS*, 529, 2208–2224), who combined Gladstone, Dewan, and Jackson-Dewan Cn² parameterizations at 1 km horizontal resolution, achieving a calibrated **seeing RMSE of 0.30 arcsec** with bias approximately −0.1 arcsec. WRF-based seeing forecasting was also validated at the Observatorio del Roque de los Muchachos by Giordano et al. (2013, *MNRAS*, 430, 3102–3111), with temperature σ = 2 K and wind speed σ = 3.9 m/s. The older **MM5** model was applied to Mauna Kea by Cherubini et al. (2008, *J. Appl. Meteor. Climatol.*, 47, 1140–1155).

### Hybrid forecasting via the autoregressive method

The most significant advance in integrating NWP output with ground-truth sensor data is the **autoregressive (AR) method** developed by the Masciadri group and implemented operationally at the **ALTA Center** (Advanced LBT Turbulence and Atmosphere) for the Large Binocular Telescope since 2019, and being extended to ESO's VLT as the **FATE** (Forecast of Atmospheric Turbulence at ESO) system. The AR technique uses Astro-Meso-NH forecasts as a long-range baseline and corrects them with real-time in-situ DIMM, MASS, and SLODAR measurements through autoregressor coefficients computed over a buffer of past nights. At the VLT, this achieves **seeing RMSE of 0.08 arcsec at a 1-hour forecast horizon**—comparable to instrumental measurement uncertainty and a factor of ~3 improvement over standard mesoscale forecasts alone (Masciadri, Turchi & Fini 2023, *MNRAS*, 523, 3487–3502). All astroclimatic parameters (seeing, τ₀, θ₀, ground-layer fraction) are forecast with RMSE at or below instrumental uncertainty. For PWV, the AR method provides an approximately eightfold gain over standard forecasts (Turchi et al. 2020, *MNRAS*, 497, 4910–4920). Related publications include Masciadri, Martelloni & Turchi (2020, *MNRAS*, 492, 140–152) and operational system descriptions in Masciadri et al. (2016, *Proc. SPIE*, 9909, 99090I; 2022, *Proc. SPIE*, 12185, 121851Q).

### Machine learning approaches at ESO facilities

Purely data-driven ML approaches at Paranal have yielded more modest results than the physics-informed AR method. Milli et al. (2019, arXiv: 1910.13767; *Proc. SPIE*, 10703, 107036I) tested **Random Forests, Multilayer Perceptrons, and LSTM networks** for two-hour seeing nowcasting using ASM data. These provided only modest improvement over persistence prediction, with the LSTM slightly outperforming MLP. The fundamental limitation is that high-frequency turbulence bursts are chaotic and poorly predicted from local time-series alone. Milli et al. (2020, *Proc. SPIE*, 11448, 114481J; arXiv: 2012.05674) subsequently exploited **spatial diversity** between Paranal and Armazones (~20 km apart), finding that high-altitude turbulence is well correlated between sites, enabling cross-site nowcasting. Turchi, Masciadri & Fini (2022, *Proc. SPIE*, 12185, 121855W; arXiv: 2210.11236) directly compared ML algorithms against the AR method at the VLT, concluding that ML techniques were "not really competitive" for turbulence forecasting alone but could serve complementary roles. A University of Chile thesis (2024) reported seeing prediction RMSE of 0.151 arcsec using extensive ML model testing, representing a 14.2% improvement over a baseline model.

For the VLT's operational Short-Term Scheduler, Anderson et al. (2024, *Proc. SPIE*, 13098, 1309805; arXiv: 2407.16049) tested a **Random Forest seeing nowcast** model using time series of seeing, coherence time, pressure, temperature, and wind, finding it matched or outperformed the "precast" approach and projected **~25 additional hours of high-priority observations per semester per telescope**. At the Canada-France-Hawaii Telescope, Gilda et al. (2022, *MNRAS*, 510, 870–902; arXiv: 2107.00048) achieved ~0.07 arcsec MAE for image quality prediction using mixture density networks and demonstrated ~12% reduction in observing time through optimal dome vent configuration.

The accuracy hierarchy across methods at Paranal is clear: **AR hybrid method (0.08 arcsec at 1 h) > ML approaches (~0.15 arcsec) > mesoscale NWP alone (~0.30 arcsec) > GCM approach (useful for profile shapes but limited for extremes)**.

## 2. The Rubin Observatory scheduling architecture

### How the Feature-Based Scheduler uses atmospheric conditions

The Rubin Observatory Legacy Survey of Space and Time (LSST) employs the **Feature-Based Scheduler (FBS)**, a fundamentally different approach from classical queue scheduling. Formalized as a **Markov Decision Process** by Naghib et al. (2019, *AJ*, 157, 151; arXiv: 1810.04815), the FBS evaluates all candidate observations at each decision point (~every 34 seconds) using a reward function computed as a weighted sum of basis functions. These basis functions encode observing desiderata including sky brightness, seeing, airmass, survey progress, and slew time penalties. An offline **Differential Evolution** optimizer tunes the basis function weights using simulation data, but the real-time scheduler is deterministic and interpretable. The earlier FBS concept was presented by Naghib, Vanderbei & Stubbs (2016, *Proc. SPIE*, 9910, 991011), and the LSST Scheduler engineering is described by Delgado & Reuter (2016, *Proc. SPIE*, 9910, 991013).

At each decision point, the scheduler ingests real-time telemetry: current **seeing** (FWHM at zenith, 500 nm), sky brightness in all six ugrizy filters, cloud cover (binary or transparency estimates from all-sky camera), wind speed and direction, and humidity. In good seeing, the scheduler preferentially selects r- and i-band observations for weak lensing science; in poor seeing or bright moonlight, it shifts to y- and z-band observations. The sky brightness model, documented by Yoachim et al. (2016, *Proc. SPIE*, 9910, 99101A), extends the ESO SkyCalc calculator with an empirical twilight model from a Canon 5D all-sky camera at Cerro Pachón, achieving typical residual RMS of **±0.2–0.3 mag arcsec⁻²**. Cloud cover history comes from ten years of CTIO night logs (1975–2005), and seeing models use **ten years of DIMM data from Cerro Pachón**.

Critically, the Rubin scheduler operates in a **purely reactive mode**—it uses current measured conditions rather than forecasting future conditions. This contrasts with ESO's VLT, which is actively developing ML-based seeing nowcasting for its Short-Term Scheduler. This represents a potential improvement area for Rubin, particularly if seeing or cloud predictions on ~1-hour timescales could improve survey efficiency.

### The OpSim simulation framework

The Operations Simulator (OpSim) generates stochastic realizations of the LSST ten-year survey, producing SQLite databases of simulated observations with complete condition characterization. The framework integrates telescope kinematic models, weather models based on historical CTIO/Cerro Pachón records, the Yoachim et al. sky brightness model, and the FBS scheduling algorithm. OpSim was originally described by Delgado et al. (2006, *Proc. SPIE*, 6270, 62701D) and substantially updated by Delgado et al. (2014, *Proc. SPIE*, 9150, 915015) and Connolly et al. (2014, *Proc. SPIE*, 9150, 915014). The current software is maintained as the `rubin_sim` and `rubin_scheduler` Python packages. The LSST Metrics Analysis Framework (MAF) enables evaluation of OpSim outputs with customizable science metrics (Jones et al. 2014, *Proc. SPIE*, 9149, 91490B).

Community-driven survey strategy optimization is documented by Bianco et al. (2022, *ApJS*, 258, 1), the opening paper of the ApJS Focus Issue on Rubin Cadence, and the earlier COSEP white paper by Marshall et al. (2017, arXiv: 1708.04058). Lochner et al. (2022, *ApJS*, 259, 58) evaluated over 100 simulated survey designs for cosmological probes. An alternative deterministic scheduler, ALTSched, was proposed by Rothchild, Stubbs & Yoachim (2019, *PASP*, 131, 115002; arXiv: 1903.00531), providing a performance lower bound. The definitive LSST system overview is Ivezić et al. (2019, *ApJ*, 873, 111).

## 3. TMT and ELT site selection: forecasting in the field

### Instruments and methods in the TMT site testing campaign

The TMT site testing campaign (2003–2008) deployed standardized instrument suites at five candidate sites: Cerros Tolar, Armazones, and Tolonchar in Chile, San Pedro Mártir in Mexico, and 13 North (13N) on Mauna Kea. The comprehensive program is documented in a series of *PASP* papers: Schöck et al. (2009, *PASP*, 121, 384–395; arXiv: 0904.1183) provide the overview; Els et al. (2009, *PASP*, 121, 527–543; arXiv: 0904.1865) present turbulence profiles; Skidmore et al. (2009, *PASP*, 121, 1151–1166) cover seeing and isoplanatic angle statistics; and Travouillon et al. (2009, *PASP*, 121, 787–796) address coherence time. Instrumentation at each site included **MASS-DIMM** on 35 cm telescopes atop 6.5 m towers, **SODAR** (both low-range SFAS at 10–200 m and high-range XFAS at 40–800 m from Scintec), automated weather stations, all-sky cameras (ASCAs), dust sensors, tower-mounted thermal probes, and IRMA infrared radiometers for water vapor. SODAR calibration is documented by Travouillon (2006, *Proc. SPIE*, 6267, 626720) and cross-validated by Travouillon et al. (2011, *Boundary-Layer Meteorology*), reporting wind speed systematic differences of 9–12% and optical turbulence systematic differences of 3–6%.

Key findings include that the **lowest 200 m is the dominant source of seeing**, Chilean sites show weaker ground layers, and 13N has the weakest 16 km turbulence yielding the largest isoplanatic angle. A multi-dimensional site merit function was developed (Schöck et al. 2011, *RevMexAA Conf. Ser.*, 41, 32) to reduce the multi-parameter dataset to a single decision metric.

### ESO ELT site characterization

The ESO ELT site evaluation campaign assessed Armazones, Ventarrones, Tolonchar, and Vizcachas in Chile alongside La Palma/ORM in Spain, Aklim in Morocco, and Macón in Argentina. The site characterization series in *PASP* includes: Vernin et al. (2011, *PASP*, 123, 1334–1346) for the overview; Vázquez Ramió et al. (2012, *PASP*, 124) for high angular resolution parameters; and Varela et al. (2014, *PASP*, 126, 412–431) for ground meteorology. Identical instrument suites (IAC-DIMM, MASS-DIMM, automated weather stations) were deployed at all sites. For Armazones specifically, Sarazin et al. (2013, *Proc. AO4ELT3*) combined long-term MASS statistics (2010–2012) with short-term high-resolution Paranal SCIDAR campaigns to build 35-layer reference Cn² profiles for ELT adaptive optics design. Seidel et al. (2016, *A&A*) derived six years of PWV data from the BESO spectrograph at Cerro Armazones, including El Niño effects.

### Satellite, reanalysis, and profiling data integration

Site pre-selection relied heavily on satellite data. Erasmus & Sarazin (2000, *Proc. SPIE*, 4168) developed methods using GOES 6.7 μm and 10.7 μm channels for PWV and cirrus cloud cover estimation. Cavazzani et al. (2011, *MNRAS*, 411, 1271) used MERIS/Envisat at 1 km resolution, validated against ESO ground measurements at La Silla and Paranal. Erasmus & van Rooyen (2006, ESO Report) analyzed seven years of Meteosat data for ELT candidate sites in Morocco and Spain.

**ERA5 reanalysis data** has become a standard tool for site characterization. Bi et al. (2024, *MNRAS*, 527, 4616–4631) applied Dewan and HMNSP99 turbulence models to 21 years of ERA5 data for the Lenghu site, achieving temperature bias < 1.5 K and wind speed bias < 2 m/s versus radiosondes. Zhou et al. (2026, *MNRAS*, 545) combined ERA5 with ARIMA and Holt-Winters forecasting at the Daocheng site. He et al. (2021, *MNRAS*, 501, 4692) validated ERA5 wind and temperature profiles against radiosondes for Tibetan Plateau sites.

Turbulence profiling techniques range from balloon-borne microthermal sensors (Trinquet & Vernin 2007, *Environ. Fluid Mech.*, 7, 397) to the optical methods. The original SCIDAR was proposed by Vernin & Roddier (1973, *JOSA*, 63, 270), with Generalized SCIDAR by Fuchs, Tallon & Vernin (1998), and the modern Stereo-SCIDAR by Shepherd et al. (2014, *MNRAS*, 437, 3568–3577). SLODAR was introduced by Wilson (2002, *MNRAS*, 337, 103–108) and refined by Butterley, Wilson & Sarazin (2006, *MNRAS*, 369, 835–845). Cross-validation between instruments is documented by Tokovinin et al. (2005, *PASP*, 117, 395) and Griffiths et al. (2024, *MNRAS*, 529, 320–330), the latter comparing next-generation profilers at Paranal.

For Mauna Kea specifically, the **Mauna Kea Weather Center** has developed progressively sophisticated forecasting. Cherubini et al. (2008, *J. Appl. Meteor. Climatol.*, 47, 1140) applied MM5 for optical turbulence, Lyman et al. (2020, *MNRAS*, 496, 4734–4748) analyzed 10+ years of forecast performance finding correlation ρ~0.35 between summit wind speed and seeing, and Cherubini, Lyman & Businger (2022, *MNRAS*, 509, 232–245) applied **neural networks** trained on GFS and MASS-DIMM data for five-night-ahead seeing prediction. Climate change impacts on observatory sites are assessed by Cantalloube et al. (2020, arXiv: 2009.11779) using ERA5, ERA20C, and CMIP6 projections.

## 4. Weather automation at smaller and robotic facilities

### How robotic telescope networks handle atmospheric conditions

Robotic telescopes universally employ threshold-based weather decision systems backed by hardware safety interlocks. The **Liverpool Telescope** (2.0 m, La Palma) uses a dedicated Weather Monitoring System based on Vaisala QLI50 sensors measuring air temperature, humidity, dew point, wind, insolation, rain/dew, and 10 μm sky temperature for cloud sensing. Its Robotic Control System implements an **Environment Monitoring Architecture** (EMA) with configurable rule-based decision-making (Steele et al. 2004, *Proc. SPIE*, 5489, 679–692; Fraser & Steele 2002, *Proc. SPIE*, 4848, 443–454). The **Las Cumbres Observatory** network operates a centralized AI-based global adaptive scheduler that evaluates weather at all seven sites simultaneously and reroutes observations to the next available telescope upon weather failure (Brown et al. 2013, *PASP*, 125, 1031–1055; Saunders et al. 2014, *Hotwiring the Transient Universe IV*, 117).

The **BOOTES** network uses multi-level decision models with IR cloud sensors, rain detectors, wind and humidity sensors, plus CASANDRA all-sky cameras, operating under RTS2 control since 2004 (Castro-Tirado 2010, *Adv. Astron.*, 570489; Hu et al. 2023, *Front. Astron. Space Sci.*, 10, 952887). **TAROT** telescopes at Calern and La Silla achieve ~80% availability through fully autonomous scheduling (Klotz et al. 2008, *PASP*, 120, 1298). The **MASTER** network uses Boltwood Cloud Sensors with a clear-sky threshold of sky-ambient temperature differential of **−21°C** (−19°C at highland sites), validated against photometric conditions (Kornilov et al. 2012, *Exp. Astron.*, 33, 173–209). The **REM** 60 cm telescope at La Silla (Zerbi et al. 2002, *Proc. SPIE*, 4841, 737–748) benefits from site infrastructure, while **ROTSE-III** established paradigms for fully autonomous closed-loop robotic telescope operation (Akerlof et al. 2003, *PASP*, 115, 132–140).

### Observatory control frameworks and safety systems

The dominant open-source frameworks for weather-integrated observatory control are:

- **RTS2** (Remote Telescope System 2nd Version): A plug-and-play architecture where each weather sensor votes for bad weather via state bits, triggering controlled dome closure. PLC-based roof control operates independently of the computer as a hardware failsafe (Kubánek 2010, *Adv. Astron.*, 902484; Kubánek et al. 2006, *Proc. SPIE*, 6274). Installed at ~12 observatories including BOOTES, FRAM, and BART.
- **INDI** (Instrument Neutral Distributed Interface): Provides standardized weather drivers including `indi_openweathermap_weather` for OpenWeatherMap API, `indi_vantage_weather` for Davis stations, and `indi_aagsolo_weather` for AAG CloudWatcher. Each driver reports parameters with OK/WARNING/ALERT states that trigger observatory shutdown via the Ekos/KStars scheduler.
- **pyobs**: A Python framework with a `pyobs-weather` module that aggregates data from multiple stations, currently running MONET/North, MONET/South, and IAG 50 cm telescopes (Husser et al. 2022, *Front. Astron. Space Sci.*, 9, 891486; arXiv: 2203.12642).
- **ASCOM**: Provides Safety Monitor and Observing Conditions interfaces compatible with commercial sensors (Boltwood, AAG, SkyAlert) and automation software (MaxIm DL, NINA, ACP).

Low-cost weather monitoring has been documented using **AAG CloudWatcher** IR thermopile-based cloud detectors, **Boltwood Cloud Sensors** calibrated against photometric standards at the Liverpool Telescope (Marchant, Smith & Steele 2008, *Proc. SPIE*, 7012, 70123U), **Davis Vantage Pro** stations, and increasingly **Arduino/Raspberry Pi systems** using MLX90614 IR sensors for cloud detection and RG-11 rain sensors. The **7Timer!** service provides GFS-based astronomical weather forecasts at ~20 km resolution globally, validated against ground truth at multiple observatories (Ye, arXiv: 1011.3863). The **Clear Sky Chart** by Rahill and Danko uses the Canadian GEM model, reporting ~85% accuracy for mostly-clear forecasts at < 12-hour horizons.

### Emerging ML applications for observatory safety

Deep learning for cloud detection from all-sky camera imagery is advancing rapidly. A study at the Iranian National Observatory achieved **99% accuracy for precipitation classification** using EfficientNet-b7 on ~2,445 all-sky images (2025, *Machine Learning with Applications*; arXiv: 2503.18670). PSO-optimized XGBoost applied to 48,870 images from Muztagh Observatory achieved **96.91% classification accuracy** using 19 features including sky background and star density (2024, *Electronics*, 13, 1503). For short-term cloud prediction, optical flow-based tracking of all-sky camera sequences enables 15–30 minute forecast horizons, being developed for the New Robotic Telescope look-ahead scheduler (2025, *RAS Techniques and Instruments*; arXiv: 2507.21711). University-class automation examples include the George Mason 0.8 m telescope using Python-based TESS follow-up with ASCOM weather integration (2022, *JATIS*, 8, 027002) and the pt5m 0.5 m robotic telescope on La Palma (Hardy et al. 2015, *MNRAS*, 454, 4316–4334).

## 5. Sky Quality Meters in operational and monitoring contexts

### Deployment, calibration, and known limitations

The Unihedron Sky Quality Meter has become the de facto standard for ground-based night sky brightness (NSB) measurements, with deployments ranging from professional observatories to citizen science networks. Comprehensive site characterization using SQM-equivalent photometry at Calar Alto established benchmark values of **22.01 mag/arcsec² in V-band** at zenith (Sánchez et al. 2007, *PASP*, 119, 1186–1200; Sánchez et al. 2008, *PASP*, 120, 1244). The ASTMON all-sky multi-band camera extends SQM-like monitoring to full-sky extinction and cloud mapping (Aceituno et al. 2011, *PASP*, 123, 1076–1086). The first technical characterizations were by Cinzano (2005, 2007, ISTIL Internal Reports). The SQM-L variant has a ~20° FWHM field versus ~84° for the original SQM.

Inter-device calibration is a persistent challenge. Den Outer et al. (2011, *Sensors*, 11, 9603–9612) found initial scatter of **±14%** across nine SQMs, reducible to 0.5% after intercalibration. Temperature stability was confirmed by Schnitt et al. (2013, *Sensors*, 13, 12166–12174) across −15°C to 35°C. Absolute radiometric calibration procedures for SQM and TESS-W were established by Bará, Tapia & Zamorano (2019, *Sensors*, 19, 1336). Critically, Sánchez de Miguel et al. (2017, *MNRAS*, 467, 2966–2979) demonstrated that as a broadband detector, **SQM can hide up to a 261% increase in light pollution** during HPS-to-LED lamp transitions, motivating multi-band alternatives. Long-term sensor degradation of **29–86 mmag/arcsec²/year** requires twilight-based ageing corrections (Bertolo et al. 2022, *Remote Sensing*, 14, 5787; Fiorentin et al. 2025, *Sensors*, 25, 516).

Approximate SQM-to-condition mappings place **21.7–22.0 mag/arcsec²** at natural dark sky (Bortle 1–2), 20.0–21.0 at the rural-suburban transition (Bortle 4–5), and < 18.0 at heavily polluted urban sky (Bortle 8–9). The conversion to physical units follows L (cd/m²) ≈ 10.8 × 10⁴ × 10^(−0.4 × m_SQM), with **21.6 mag/arcsec² ≈ 250 μcd/m²** as a natural reference (Hänel et al. 2018, *JQSRT*, 205, 278–290—the definitive review of NSB measurement methodology). Formal relationships between mag/arcsec² and luminance are derived by Bará (2017, *Int. J. Sustain. Light.*). Pun (2022, *MNRAS*, 511, 5683–5695) used PCA on 53 sky brightness metrics from U.S. National Park Service data to identify moderate-to-strong correlations between SQM, Bortle scale, and naked-eye limiting magnitude.

### Light pollution monitoring networks and long-term trends

SQM data form the calibration backbone of the **New World Atlas of Artificial Night Sky Brightness** by Falchi et al. (2016, *Science Advances*, 2, e1600377), which combined VIIRS DNB satellite data with over 35,000 ground-based SQM observations. The **Globe at Night** citizen science network has accumulated over 100,000 SQM-LE measurements from 115+ countries since 2014. The EU H2020 **STARS4ALL** project developed the **TESS-W** photometer using a TSL237 sensor with a dichroic filter wider to the red than SQM's HOYA CM-500, incorporating WiFi/MQTT communication and a built-in IR sensor for cloud detection, deployed in a network exceeding 100 instruments (Zamorano et al. 2016, *Int. J. Sustain. Light.*, 35, 49–54). The Spanish REECL network contributed calibration data for the World Atlas (Zamorano et al. 2015, IAU FM 21).

Kyba et al. (2015, *Scientific Reports*, 5, 8409) analyzed SQM-LE time-series from over 110 worldwide locations, revealing that skyglow varies over four orders of magnitude and overcast skies range from one-third darker to **18 times brighter** than clear sky depending on light pollution levels. Regional networks include the Austrian 26-site network (Posch et al. 2018, *JQSRT*, 211, 144–165), the Veneto network coordinated by ARPA-Veneto and University of Padova (Bertolo et al. 2019, *J. Imaging*, 5, 56), studies at Vienna and Potsdam (Puschnig et al. 2014, *JQSRT*, 139, 64–75 and 76–81), and the Hong Kong assessment finding urban skies at **~15.0 mag/arcsec²** (Pun & So 2012, *Environ. Monit. Assess.*, 184, 2537). The most comprehensive long-term trend analysis to date is by Puschnig et al. (2023, *MNRAS*, 518, 4449–4465), using 4–10 year SQM time-series at 26 locations with atmospheric modeling to isolate anthropogenic trends from natural variability.

### SQM for cloud detection, transparency, and ML applications

Cavazzani et al. (2020, *MNRAS*, 493, 2463–2471) demonstrated SQM as a reliable operational cloud detector at professional observatories, achieving **97.2% correlation** with GOES satellite cloud detection at La Silla and 94.6% at Asiago/Ekar. The method exploits the fact that clouds amplify skyglow near cities (urban sites become brighter under clouds) while darkening truly dark sites. This urban cloud amplification effect was quantified by Kyba et al. (2011, *PLoS ONE*, 6, e17307; 2012, *MNRAS*, 425, 701–708) and spatially mapped by Jechow et al. (2017, *Scientific Reports*, 7, 6741). Despite this demonstrated accuracy, no publications describe formal integration of SQM cloud detection into automated observatory scheduling optimization systems.

Machine learning applied to SQM time-series remains nascent. **Priyatikanto (2020, *Adv. Astron.*, 5102065)** presented the first ML classification model for SQM data, using Random Forest to classify nightly light curves into six categories (twilight, overcast, cloudy, clear, cloudy-moonlit, clear-moonlit) from eight Indonesian stations. A 2025 study in *Remote Sensing* (17, 1332) applied voting ensemble models to SQM observations from China and Belgium combined with VIIRS and cloud remote sensing data for NSB prediction and local-scale mapping. No LSTM, Transformer, or deep learning architectures specifically targeting SQM time-series forecasting were identified in the literature, representing a clear gap.

## Conclusion: converging toward integrated intelligent systems

Several cross-cutting themes emerge from this review. First, the **hierarchy of forecasting approaches** is now well established: physics-informed hybrid methods (NWP + real-time sensor correction via autoregression) consistently outperform both pure NWP and pure ML approaches for atmospheric turbulence prediction, with the AR method achieving instrumental-uncertainty-level accuracy at 1-hour horizons. Second, a **reactive-vs-predictive divide** separates current operational systems: Rubin/LSST's FBS makes greedy decisions based on current conditions, while ESO's FATE system and the Mauna Kea Weather Center actively forecast future states. Third, **open-source observatory control frameworks** (RTS2, INDI, pyobs, ASCOM) have matured to provide standardized weather integration for facilities of all sizes, though decision-making remains predominantly rule-based at smaller observatories. Fourth, ML for cloud detection from all-sky imagery has reached operational accuracy (96–99%), while ML for turbulence prediction from local sensor data alone remains limited by the chaotic nature of atmospheric turbulence. Fifth, SQM networks provide robust cloud detection (97% satellite correlation) and invaluable light pollution monitoring but lack integration into scheduling algorithms and deep learning exploitation of their time-series data. These gaps—particularly ML-augmented scheduling at Rubin, deep learning for SQM forecasting, and wider deployment of hybrid NWP+sensor systems beyond the largest observatories—define the frontier for the next generation of intelligent observatory operations.
