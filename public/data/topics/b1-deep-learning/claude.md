# Deep Learning Architectures for Meteorological Time Series Forecasting

## 1. Evolution of deep learning for time series forecasting

The application of deep learning to meteorological time series forecasting has undergone a rapid and transformative evolution over three decades, progressing from simple recurrent architectures to billion-parameter foundation models that now rival — and in many cases surpass — physics-based numerical weather prediction (NWP) systems. This chapter traces the key architectural developments, evaluates their application to weather and climate data, and identifies the current state of the art alongside open challenges.

### From recurrent networks to the Transformer paradigm

The foundational architecture for sequential data modelling was the **Recurrent Neural Network (RNN)**, introduced by Elman (1990), which maintained a hidden state updated at each time step. While conceptually suited to time series, vanilla RNNs suffered from the vanishing gradient problem, making it impossible to learn dependencies spanning more than approximately 10–20 time steps — a critical limitation for meteorological data where seasonal cycles, synoptic-scale patterns, and teleconnections operate over timescales of days to years.

The **Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM)** network, proposed by Hochreiter and Schmidhuber (1997, "Long Short-Term Memory," *Neural Computation*, 9(8), 1735–1780), addressed this limitation through a gated cell architecture comprising forget, input, and output gates that regulate information flow through a persistent cell state. This mechanism enabled LSTMs to capture long-range temporal dependencies — a property directly relevant to meteorological forecasting where variables exhibit multi-scale temporal correlations from diurnal cycles to interannual oscillations. The LSTM remained the dominant architecture for sequence modelling for nearly two decades.

The **Gated Recurrent Unit (GRU)**, introduced by Cho et al. (2014, "Learning Phrase Representations using RNN Encoder-Decoder for Statistical Machine Translation," *Proceedings of EMNLP*), simplified the LSTM by merging the cell state and hidden state and reducing the gate count from three to two (reset and update gates). The GRU achieved comparable performance to the LSTM on many tasks with fewer parameters and faster training, making it particularly attractive for resource-constrained meteorological applications and real-time forecasting systems.

The introduction of **attention mechanisms** by Bahdanau et al. (2015, "Neural Machine Translation by Jointly Learning to Align and Translate," *ICLR*) represented a paradigm shift. Rather than compressing an entire input sequence into a fixed-length vector, attention allowed the decoder to dynamically focus on relevant portions of the input at each prediction step. For meteorological applications, this meant that models could learn to attend to specific historical time steps — such as the same season in previous years or the onset of a particular synoptic pattern — when making predictions, substantially improving both accuracy and interpretability.

The **Transformer architecture**, proposed by Vaswani et al. (2017, "Attention Is All You Need," *NeurIPS*), eliminated recurrence entirely in favour of multi-head self-attention operating over the full sequence in parallel. This brought two critical advantages: first, the ability to capture arbitrarily long-range dependencies without the sequential bottleneck of RNNs; second, massive parallelisation during training, enabling models to scale to unprecedented sizes. The Transformer's self-attention mechanism computes pairwise relationships between all positions in a sequence simultaneously, with computational complexity O(L²) where L is the sequence length.

The period **2022–2025** has witnessed the emergence of **weather foundation models** — large-scale deep learning systems pre-trained on decades of global reanalysis data that can produce medium-range weather forecasts in seconds rather than the hours required by traditional NWP. Models such as Pangu-Weather (Bi et al., 2023), GraphCast (Lam et al., 2023), and GenCast (Price et al., 2024) have demonstrated forecast skill exceeding that of the European Centre for Medium-Range Weather Forecasts (ECMWF) Integrated Forecasting System (IFS) across the majority of verification targets, marking what many researchers consider a watershed moment in atmospheric science.

### Why each advance mattered for meteorological data

Each architectural breakthrough addressed specific challenges inherent to weather time series. LSTMs enabled modelling of seasonal and synoptic-scale memory — critical for capturing how atmospheric states evolve over days to weeks. GRUs provided computational efficiency for operational forecasting where inference latency matters. Attention mechanisms allowed models to learn physically meaningful temporal relationships, such as the influence of El Niño–Southern Oscillation phases on regional weather patterns months later. Transformers unlocked the ability to process extremely long input sequences (hundreds to thousands of time steps) without information loss, essential for climate-scale modelling. Foundation models leveraged the Transformer's scalability to learn general-purpose atmospheric representations from petabytes of reanalysis data, enabling transfer learning and fine-tuning for specialised tasks.

## 2. LSTM and variants in weather forecasting

### The standard LSTM architecture and its suitability for weather data

The LSTM cell (Hochreiter and Schmidhuber, 1997) operates through three gating mechanisms. The **forget gate** f_t = σ(W_f · [h_{t−1}, x_t] + b_f) determines what proportion of the cell state to retain — critical for weather data where some seasonal patterns persist over months while short-term fluctuations should be discarded. The **input gate** i_t = σ(W_i · [h_{t−1}, x_t] + b_i) controls what new information is stored, enabling the network to incorporate sudden meteorological shifts such as cold front passages or rapid pressure drops. The **output gate** o_t = σ(W_o · [h_{t−1}, x_t] + b_o) regulates what information propagates to the next time step. The **cell state** C_t acts as a long-term memory highway, enabling the capture of annual temperature cycles, multi-scale dependencies, and long-range teleconnections that characterise atmospheric variability.

These properties make the LSTM particularly well-suited to meteorological time series, where the data exhibit non-stationarity, multi-scale periodicity (diurnal, synoptic, seasonal, interannual), abrupt regime transitions, and complex nonlinear relationships between variables. However, LSTMs face limitations: performance degrades for very long-range predictions (beyond approximately 48 hours for hourly data), they require substantial training datasets to avoid overfitting, and they offer limited interpretability compared to statistical models (Teixeira et al., 2024, "Enhancing Weather Forecasting Integrating LSTM and GA," *Applied Sciences*, 14(13), 5769).

### Stacked LSTM versus single-layer LSTM

Stacking multiple LSTM layers creates a deep recurrent architecture capable of learning hierarchical representations at different levels of temporal abstraction. Agrawal et al. (2020, "Big Data Analytics Using Deep LSTM Networks: A Case Study for Weather Prediction," *ASTESJ*, 5(2)) evaluated stacked architectures using 15 years of hourly data from 122 Brazilian weather stations (~9.7 million records). Their key finding was that **two hidden layers provided optimal accuracy**, with the Adam optimiser outperforming SGD and RMSProp. Adding a third layer did not improve accuracy, as the network began learning approximately linear relationships. A comparative study on 48-hour temperature prediction in Santiago, Brazil (using INMET data) confirmed that stacked LSTMs reduce average absolute error by approximately 2°C relative to vanilla single-layer LSTMs, though both architectures struggled with secondary temperature valleys and unexpected fluctuations.

Wang et al. (2024, *ICIAAI Proceedings*) proposed a four-layer stacked LSTM with 50 units per layer and dropout regularisation, demonstrating that deeper stacking enables the learning of more diverse abstract temporal features. Fathima and Kovoor (2025, "Explainable AI Insights into a Time Series Weather Prediction Model Using Stacked LSTM," *Lecture Notes in Networks and Systems*, vol. 1165, Springer) applied SHAP-based explainability analysis to stacked LSTMs for temperature prediction, revealing that the hierarchical feature extraction corresponds to physically interpretable temporal scales.

### Bidirectional LSTM for meteorological post-processing

Bidirectional LSTMs (BiLSTMs) process sequences in both forward and backward directions simultaneously, concatenating outputs from both passes to capture richer temporal context. This architecture is particularly effective for NWP post-processing, where the full temporal context of a forecast window is available at inference time.

Kim et al. (2021, "Temperature Prediction Based on Bidirectional LSTM and CNN Combining Observed and Numerical Forecast Data," *Sensors*, 21(4), 1397) combined BiLSTM with CNN for temperature prediction using Korean Meteorological Agency automated weather station data and RDAPS NWP forecasts at 12 km resolution. The model predicted temperature up to **336 hours (14 days)** in advance, with the CNN processing 2D NWP image data while the BiLSTM handled 1D observed time series, effectively performing learned NWP post-processing. Zhang et al. (2024, "CNN-BiLSTM-AM for Severe Convective Precipitation," *Atmosphere*, 15(10), 1229) integrated CNN, BiLSTM, and attention for precipitation forecasting using ERA5 hourly data, demonstrating that the model outperformed ConvLSTM, PredRNN++, CNN, FC-LSTM, and standard LSTM, with the performance advantage increasing at longer lead times. For wind speed forecasting, an optimised BiLSTM using genetic and dipper-throated optimisation achieved RMSE = 0.00046 (normalised) with NSE = 0.997 (Frontiers in Energy Research, 2023).

The principal limitation of BiLSTMs is their **doubled computational cost** relative to unidirectional LSTMs. They require the full input sequence to be available before processing, making them unsuitable for true real-time streaming applications but highly effective for reanalysis correction and retrospective forecast calibration.

### LSTM with attention mechanisms

The integration of attention with LSTM addresses a fundamental limitation of standard recurrent architectures: the tendency to weight recent time steps disproportionately, regardless of their predictive relevance. Suleman and Shridevi (2022, "Short-Term Weather Forecasting Using Spatial Feature Attention Based LSTM Model," *IEEE Access*, 10, 82456–82468) proposed an encoder-decoder architecture with spatial feature attention applied to 87,620 hourly observations from Environment Canada, providing **interpretable spatial feature weights** that reveal which input variables most influence predictions. Dong et al. (2025, "TA-LSTM: Temporal Attention LSTM for Spatiotemporal Weather Prediction," *Multimedia Systems*, 31, 466) developed a Temporal Attention Module that extends the temporal receptive field by assigning varying attention levels to different historical time steps, directly addressing LSTM's limited performance in capturing long-term temporal correlations. Li et al. (2022, "RSDF-AM-LSTM: Regional Scale Division Rainfall Forecasting Using Attention and LSTM," *ACM/IMS Trans. Data Sci.*, 2(4), Article 35) applied spatial and temporal attention to data from **3,200 meteorological stations** in China, using a 4×3 attention matrix (4 rainfall levels × 3 time slots) that outperformed both traditional atmospheric models and other machine learning approaches.

### Application-specific LSTM studies

**Temperature prediction.** Ye et al. (2022, "Prediction of Hourly Air Temperature Based on CNN–LSTM," *Geomatics, Natural Hazards and Risk*, 13(1), 1962–1986) used 60,133 hourly observations from Yinchuan, China (January 2000 to October 2020) with six input variables, achieving **MAE = 1.02°C, RMSE = 1.97°C** with a CNN-LSTM hybrid, compared to standalone LSTM goodness-of-fit of 0.5949 and CNN-LSTM of 0.7258. The model exhibited degraded accuracy under extreme weather conditions. Guo et al. (2024, "Monthly Climate Prediction Using Deep CNN and LSTM," *Scientific Reports*, 14, 17748) evaluated 72 years of monthly data from Jinan, China, finding that CNN-LSTM achieved **RMSE = 0.6292°C** for monthly average temperature, outperforming standalone LSTM (1.3482°C), CNN (0.8015°C), RNN (1.4416°C), and ANN (2.0669°C).

**Rainfall and precipitation prediction.** Ogunjo et al. (2022, "Rainfall Prediction: A Comparative Analysis of Modern Machine Learning Algorithms," *Machine Learning with Applications*, 7, 100204) compared LSTM variants on climate data from five UK cities (2000–2020), finding that stacked LSTM and BiLSTM performed best. Mehdizadeh et al. (2022, "Forecast of Rainfall Distribution Based on Fixed Sliding Window LSTM," *Engineering Applications of Computational Fluid Mechanics*, 16(1)) evaluated monthly rainfall from Rize and Konya stations in Turkey, reporting that LSTM improved RMSE estimates by **12.2–14.9%** and NSE estimates by **32.9–33.2%** over Random Forest. A notable failure case is reported by Sarwade et al. (2024, "Predictive Modeling of Extreme Weather Forecasting Events," *Current World Environment*, 19(1)), where LSTM achieved only R² = 0.235 for rainfall prediction at Kolhapur, India, illustrating the persistent difficulty of precipitation forecasting even with deep learning.

**Cloud cover forecasting.** Nielsen et al. (2022, "Deep Learning Based Short-Term Total Cloud Cover Forecasting," *IEEE IGARSS*, 9892773) compared LSTM, BiLSTM, and CNN-LSTM for total cloud cover prediction from satellite imagery, achieving average RMSE of **0.054 (first half of daytime) and 0.082 (full daytime)**. Kim et al. (2024, "Application of Deep Learning in Cloud Cover Prediction Using Geostationary Satellite Images," *GIScience & Remote Sensing*, 61(1), 2440506) found that ConvLSTM consistently outperformed standalone LSTM for binary cloud detection using GEO-KOMPSAT-2A data at 2 km spatial resolution and 10-minute temporal resolution. Berthomier et al. (2020, "Cloud Cover Nowcasting with Deep Learning," *arXiv:2009.11577*, Météo-France) tested 20 architectures on Meteosat satellite images for 90-minute cloud cover nowcasting, finding that while all deep learning models outperformed persistence, U-Net surpassed both LSTM variants and the AROME NWP model.

**Atmospheric seeing prediction.** Ni et al. (2022, "Data-driven Seeing Prediction for Optics Telescope: From Statistical Modeling, Machine Learning to Deep Learning Techniques," *Research in Astronomy and Astrophysics*, 22, 125003) conducted a systematic comparison at the Xinglong Observatory using LAMOST monitoring data (23,472 synchronised observations from 160 observation sequences at 30-second sampling). Weather data, interior temperature sensors (>200), and DIMM seeing measurements were used as inputs. LSTM achieved **RMSE ≈ 0.10 arcseconds**, comparable to GRU and slightly behind XGBoost (≈0.098), while the Transformer achieved ≈0.0998 but required approximately 500 seconds of training compared to seconds for XGBoost. Temperature difference features significantly improved all models. Liao et al. (2023, "Multistep Ahead Atmospheric Optical Turbulence Forecasting Using EMD and LSTM-based Seq2Seq Learning," *Frontiers in Physics*, 11, 1070762) applied EMD decomposition combined with Seq2Seq-LSTM to Cn² turbulence data from the China Remote Sensing Satellite Ground Station in Beijing, demonstrating significant improvement over standalone LSTM for multi-step ahead prediction, though accuracy degraded with increasing forecast horizon.

**Wind speed forecasting.** The MRGS-LSTM model (2024, "A Novel Multi-site Wind Speed Prediction Approach with Spatio-Temporal Correlation," *Frontiers in Energy Research*, 12, 1427587) combined GraphSAGE for spatial feature extraction with LSTM for temporal learning on data from 20 sites at Roscoe Wind Farm, Texas, achieving **MAE of 0.42–0.53 m/s and RMSE of 0.59–0.76 m/s**, with improvements of 15–28% over other GNN-based models. Ferreira et al. (2024, "Machine-Learning-Based Estimate of Wind Speed over Complex Terrain Using LSTM," *Wind Energy Science*, 9, 1431–1450) applied LSTM to lidar datasets from three sites in Brazil, achieving R² > 90% up to 100 m height, significantly outperforming the power-law approach at higher altitudes.

**Humidity prediction.** Guo et al. (2024, *Scientific Reports*) reported that CNN-LSTM achieved **RMSE = 0.66%, R = 0.998** for monthly average relative humidity at Jinan, China. Nagaraj and Kumar (2023, "Univariate Deep Learning Models for Prediction of Daily Average Temperature and Relative Humidity," *Journal of Earth System Science*, 132, 100) found that CNN-LSTM with the Adam optimiser was the best forecasting model for relative humidity at Chennai, India, followed by ConvLSTM.

## 3. Transformer-based models for weather and climate

### Temporal Fusion Transformer

The Temporal Fusion Transformer (TFT), introduced by Lim et al. (2021, "Temporal Fusion Transformers for Interpretable Multi-horizon Time Series Forecasting," *International Journal of Forecasting*, 37(4), 1748–1764), was among the first architectures to combine recurrent and attention-based components for time series forecasting. Its architecture features **Variable Selection Networks** that provide instance-wise feature importance through gated residual networks (GRNs), a hybrid temporal processing pipeline using LSTM encoder-decoder for local patterns combined with interpretable multi-head self-attention for long-range dependencies, and **quantile forecasting** that simultaneously outputs the 10th, 50th, and 90th percentiles for probabilistic predictions. The GRN architecture applies non-linear processing selectively: GRN(a, c) = LayerNorm(a + GLU(η₁)), where GLU(γ) = σ(W₄γ + b₄) ⊙ (W₅γ + b₅), using gated linear units to suppress unnecessary components.

For meteorological applications, TFT has been deployed for streamflow prediction across 2,610 globally distributed catchments from the Caravan dataset, outperforming standalone LSTM and Transformer models. On standard benchmarks, TFT achieved **7% lower P50 and P90 quantile losses** versus best baselines on its original evaluation datasets (electricity, traffic, volatility, retail). Its strength lies in interpretable multi-horizon probabilistic forecasting with mixed static and temporal inputs, though it underperforms newer architectures on standard point-forecasting benchmarks.

### Informer and ProbSparse self-attention

The Informer, proposed by Zhou et al. (2021, "Informer: Beyond Efficient Transformer for Long Sequence Time-Series Forecasting," *AAAI*, 35(12), 11106–11115; **AAAI 2021 Best Paper Award**), addressed the O(L²) computational complexity of vanilla self-attention through **ProbSparse self-attention**. The mechanism exploits the observation that attention score distributions are long-tailed: a KL divergence-based sparsity measure M(q_i, K) selects only the top-u queries (u = c · ln(L_K)) that contribute meaningfully, reducing complexity to **O(L log L)**. A **self-attention distilling** operation halves the feature map between encoder layers via Conv1d and MaxPool, further reducing space complexity. The generative-style decoder predicts long output sequences in a single forward pass, eliminating cumulative autoregressive error.

The Informer introduced the influential **ETT (Electricity Transformer Temperature) benchmark** datasets (ETTh1, ETTh2, ETTm1, ETTm2), which have become standard evaluation benchmarks for time series Transformers. In a comprehensive 2025 study published in *PeerJ*, the Informer achieved **RMSE of 3.19** on real-world weather data, demonstrating the best overall performance among 14 neural network models tested. However, the ProbSparse mechanism may neglect periodic features, and subsequent architectures have substantially improved upon the Informer's benchmark performance.

### Autoformer and auto-correlation

The Autoformer (Wu et al., 2021, "Autoformer: Decomposition Transformers with Auto-Correlation for Long-Term Series Forecasting," *NeurIPS*, 34, 22419–22430) introduced two architectural innovations. First, **progressive decomposition** applies series decomposition (moving average for trend extraction) as an inner block within each encoder and decoder layer, progressively separating trend and seasonal components rather than treating decomposition as a preprocessing step. Second, the **auto-correlation mechanism** replaces point-wise self-attention with series-wise connections based on periodicity, computed efficiently via FFT: R_{Q,K}(τ) = Corr(Q_t, K_{t−τ}). The top-k time delays are selected and values are rolled (shifted) by these delays, achieving O(L log L) complexity without requiring positional embeddings.

On the Weather dataset, the Autoformer achieved MSE of **0.266 at 96-step horizon and 0.419 at 720-step horizon**, representing a 38% relative improvement over prior Transformer methods across six benchmarks. Notably, the Autoformer was deployed operationally for wind speed and temperature forecasting at competition venues during the **2022 Beijing Winter Olympics**.

### FEDformer and frequency-domain attention

The FEDformer (Zhou et al., 2022, "FEDformer: Frequency Enhanced Decomposed Transformer for Long-term Series Forecasting," *ICML*, PMLR 162:27268–27286) moves attention computation to the frequency domain through two variants: FEB-f applies Fourier transforms and randomly selects s frequency components from d total (justified by the Restricted Isometry Property), while FEB-w uses wavelet decomposition for multi-scale representation. **Frequency Enhanced Attention** computes cross-attention in the frequency domain, achieving **linear O(L) complexity**. A Mixture of Experts Decomposition (MOEDecomp) uses multiple average pooling filters with data-dependent weights for adaptive trend extraction.

On the Weather dataset, FEDformer-f achieved **MSE = 0.217 at 96-step horizon**, improving upon the Autoformer's 0.266 — a relative improvement of 14.8% on multivariate benchmarks. Kolmogorov-Smirnov tests confirmed that FEDformer output distributions most closely match input distributions among tested models.

### PatchTST and patch-based tokenisation

PatchTST (Nie et al., 2023, "A Time Series is Worth 64 Words: Long-term Forecasting with Transformers," *ICLR 2023*) resolved a fundamental challenge identified by Zeng et al. (2023, "Are Transformers Effective for Time Series Forecasting?," *AAAI*), who showed that a simple linear model (DLinear) could outperform prior Transformer variants. PatchTST introduces **patch-based tokenisation**: the time series is segmented into subseries-level patches of length P with stride S (default P = 16, S = 8), converting L time steps into approximately L/S tokens. This retains local semantic information, quadratically reduces computation for a given lookback window, and enables attending to longer histories. **Channel independence** processes each univariate channel independently through a shared Transformer backbone, acting as a regulariser against overfitting on cross-channel noise.

On the Weather dataset, PatchTST achieved **MSE ≈ 0.149 at 96-step horizon** (versus FEDformer's 0.217), representing a 27.7% improvement at 336-step horizon. Self-supervised pre-training via masked patch reconstruction further improved results, and transfer learning from ETTh1 to other ETT subsets produced state-of-the-art performance. The channel independence assumption, while effective as a regulariser, deliberately ignores cross-variate correlations.

### iTransformer and inverted attention

The iTransformer (Liu et al., 2024, "iTransformer: Inverted Transformers Are Effective for Time Series Forecasting," *ICLR 2024 Spotlight*) argues that Transformers were not ineffective for time series but rather "improperly used." Its key innovation is **inverted tokenisation**: instead of embedding all variates at one time step as a temporal token, each entire time series of a variate is embedded as one variate token (R^T → R^D via MLP). Self-attention then operates across variate tokens, directly learning interpretable multivariate correlations. The feed-forward network learns nonlinear temporal representations for each variate independently. No positional embedding is needed, as sequential information is implicitly stored in the neuron permutation of the FFN.

On the Weather dataset, iTransformer achieved **MSE = 0.258 averaged across horizons {96, 192, 336, 720}**, matching or beating PatchTST on most multivariate datasets while excelling particularly on datasets with strong cross-variate correlations. It outperforms the Autoformer (0.338) and FEDformer (0.309) by substantial margins and demonstrates robust generalisation to unseen variates.

### When Transformers outperform LSTM and vice versa

The relationship between Transformer and LSTM performance is not uniformly one-directional. Transformers excel on **long sequences (>200 time steps)** due to parallel processing and global attention, and on large datasets where their higher parameter count is an asset rather than a liability. LSTMs retain advantages on **small datasets** where Transformers are prone to overfitting, at very short prediction horizons where local sequential patterns dominate, and in low-dimensional high-noise scenarios.

Pölz et al. (2024, *Water Resources Research*) compared Transformer and LSTM for karst spring discharge forecasting, finding that the Transformer performed **9% better** for springs with longer response times, while LSTM was **4% better** for springs with the shortest response times — suggesting that the optimal architecture depends on the timescale of the dominant physical process. The TFT's hybrid design, combining LSTM for local patterns with Transformer attention for long-range dependencies, consistently outperforms both pure architectures across 2,610 globally distributed catchments, indicating that architectural complementarity may be more valuable than architectural purity.

## 4. Hybrid architectures

### CNN-LSTM for local feature extraction and temporal modelling

CNN-LSTM hybrids use convolutional layers to extract local spatial or feature-level patterns from multivariate meteorological inputs, with the CNN output serving as input to LSTM layers for temporal dependency learning. This sequential pipeline leverages the CNN's ability to learn translation-invariant local features (analogous to identifying spatial patterns in radar imagery or extracting relevant combinations of input variables) while relying on the LSTM's gating mechanisms for temporal memory.

Guo et al. (2024, *Scientific Reports*) demonstrated that CNN-LSTM reduced monthly temperature prediction RMSE from 1.35°C (standalone LSTM) to **0.63°C** at Jinan, China — a 53% improvement attributable to the CNN's spatial feature extraction. A multi-step forecasting study (2025, *Water Resources Management*, Springer) evaluated CNN-LSTM with decomposition methods (SD and EEMD) across three regions in Rio de Janeiro, finding that SD-CNN-LSTM was superior for single-step forecasts while EEMD-CNN-LSTM provided greater stability for multi-step predictions. The CNN-LSTM architecture for rainfall prediction under sliding windows (2025, *European Journal of Remote Sensing*) confirmed that incorporating atmospheric indicator information through CNN feature extraction significantly improves prediction at stations in Yichun and Zhangye, China.

The typical improvement of CNN-LSTM over pure LSTM ranges from **10–53%** in RMSE across studies, with the largest gains observed for multivariate inputs where the CNN can learn informative cross-variable feature combinations.

### ConvLSTM for spatiotemporal weather data

The Convolutional LSTM (ConvLSTM), introduced by Shi et al. (2015, "Convolutional LSTM Network: A Machine Learning Approach for Precipitation Nowcasting," *NeurIPS*, 28, 802–810; **cited over 5,500 times**), is arguably the most influential hybrid architecture for spatiotemporal meteorological data. The key innovation replaces fully connected operations in both input-to-state and state-to-state transitions with convolutional operations. All inputs, cell outputs, hidden states, and gates become **3D tensors** (channels × rows × columns), preserving the spatial structure of gridded weather data. The future state of each grid cell is determined by inputs and past states of its local neighbours via convolutional kernels.

The original evaluation used radar echo data from Hong Kong (top 97 rainy days from 2011–2013), demonstrating that ConvLSTM outperformed both FC-LSTM and the operational ROVER optical-flow algorithm for precipitation nowcasting. Subsequent work includes Convcast (2020, *PLOS ONE*), which achieved **accuracy of 0.93 at 30-minute lead** and 0.87 at 150-minute lead using NASA IMERG precipitation data; and Chen et al. (2020, *Earth and Space Science*), who enhanced ConvLSTM with a Star-Shaped Bridge for improved information flow and group normalisation for high-resolution radar images, addressing the persistent problem of heavy rainfall underestimation.

### PredRNN and spatiotemporal memory flow

PredRNN (Wang et al., 2017, "PredRNN: Recurrent Neural Networks for Predictive Learning Using Spatiotemporal LSTMs," *NeurIPS*, 30, 879–888) advanced beyond ConvLSTM through **Spatiotemporal LSTM (ST-LSTM) units** that extract spatial and temporal representations simultaneously. The key architectural innovation is a **zigzag memory flow**: memory states propagate in two directions — across stacked RNN layers vertically and through all RNN states horizontally — enabling richer information exchange than ConvLSTM's purely upward vertical propagation.

PredRNN++ (Wang et al., 2018, "PredRNN++: Towards A Resolution of the Deep-in-Time Dilemma in Spatiotemporal Predictive Learning," *ICML*, PMLR 80:5123–5132) introduced the **Causal LSTM** with cascaded dual memories and a **Gradient Highway Unit** that provides alternative routes for gradient flow, resolving the trade-off between deeper temporal transitions (needed for complex motion modelling) and vanishing gradients. PredRNN-v2 (Wang et al., 2021) further enhanced the architecture with curriculum learning for long-term dynamics. In benchmarks on precipitation nowcasting in the Helan Mountain area (2017–2020 radar data), PredRNN and PredRNN++ demonstrated the best performance among recurrent-based models, though recurrent-free models such as SimVP achieve comparable accuracy with approximately **5× fewer FLOPs and 7× higher inference speed** (OpenSTL Benchmark).

### Graph neural networks for weather station networks

Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) address a fundamental limitation of grid-based architectures: weather stations are irregularly distributed, and their spatial relationships cannot be efficiently captured by convolutional kernels designed for regular grids. GNNs model stations as nodes in a graph with edges representing spatial relationships (geographical proximity, correlation structure), enabling message-passing that aggregates information from neighbouring stations.

Keisler (2022, "Forecasting Global Weather with Graph Neural Networks," *arXiv:2202.07575*) demonstrated a GNN that steps the 3D atmospheric state forward by 6 hours, achieving test performance on Z500 and T850 **comparable to operational GFS and ECMWF** at 1-degree resolution — a pioneering result establishing GNNs as viable global weather models. HiSTGNN (2023, "Hierarchical Spatio-temporal Graph Neural Network for Weather Forecasting," *Information Sciences*) introduced a hierarchical graph with local graphs (nodes represent meteorological variables per station) and a global graph (nodes represent stations), using adaptive graph learning with dilated inception networks and GCN, achieving **4.2–11.6% error reduction** versus state-of-the-art methods. A multi-modal GNN (2024, *arXiv:2410.12938*) used heterogeneous node types for grid points and station observations across 358 weather stations in the northeastern US (ERA5 + MADIS observations, 2019–2023), addressing the critical limitation that most ML weather models produce only gridded output rather than station-specific forecasts.

### Temporal Convolutional Networks as recurrent alternatives

Temporal Convolutional Networks (TCNs), evaluated systematically by Bai et al. (2018, "An Empirical Evaluation of Generic Convolutional and Recurrent Networks for Sequence Modeling," *arXiv:1803.01271*), use **dilated causal convolutions** with exponentially increasing dilation factors (d = 1, 2, 4, 8...) to achieve exponentially large receptive fields without increasing parameter count. The causal constraint ensures outputs depend only on current and past inputs. Residual connections with 1×1 convolutions enable deep architectures with stable gradient flow.

Hewage et al. (2020, "Temporal Convolutional Neural Network for Effective Weather Forecasting," *Soft Computing*, 24, 16453–16482) applied MISO-TCN to local weather station data, finding that TCN outperformed LSTM for short-term prediction across temperature, humidity, wind, and pressure, with accuracy degrading beyond 9–12 hours for pressure predictions. The Pyramid TCN (2023, *Frontiers*) addressed standard TCN's limitation of using only last-layer features by merging multi-scale temporal features from different layers, outperforming WRF, LSTM Encoder-Decoder, and standard TCN on Beijing meteorological data. TCNs offer **30–50% faster training** than LSTMs due to full parallelisation, with comparable or slightly better accuracy and longer effective memory — making them a compelling alternative for applications where training efficiency is critical.

## 5. Weather foundation models reshape the forecasting paradigm

### Pangu-Weather: the first AI model to surpass operational NWP

Pangu-Weather (Bi et al., 2023, "Accurate Medium-Range Global Weather Forecasting with 3D Neural Networks," *Nature*, 619(7970), 533–538) was the first AI system to outperform the ECMWF IFS (HRES) in **deterministic forecasts across all tested variables at all lead times** from 1 hour to 7 days, measured by latitude-weighted RMSE and ACC. The architecture employs a **3D Earth-Specific Transformer** based on the Swin Transformer with two innovations: a 3D attention mechanism that processes pressure-level variables as a volumetric field rather than treating each level independently, and an Earth-Specific Positional Bias (ESB) with approximately 41 million learnable parameters per lead time. However, an ablation study by To et al. (2024, *GMD*) found that ESB and the 3D attention are "noninfluential" to overall performance; the Swin backbone primarily drives success.

Training used **ERA5 reanalysis (1979–2017)** at 0.25° × 0.25° spatial resolution (~28 km), with 5 surface variables and 5 atmospheric variables at 13 pressure levels. A hierarchical temporal strategy employs four separate models at 1h, 3h, 6h, and 24h lead times; a greedy algorithm combines them for any forecast horizon with minimal autoregressive steps. Total training required approximately 73,000 GPU hours on NVIDIA V100s. Pangu-Weather accurately predicted Typhoon Mawar's trajectory 5 days in advance, and ECMWF integrated it into their experimental operational suite in July 2023. Xia et al. (2025) demonstrated fine-tuning via IFS analysis data for improved regional performance in South China.

The primary limitations are the **regression bias toward smoothed spatial-temporal averages** that suppresses extreme values, weaker performance for mesoscale severe convective events and tropical cyclone intensity, and the absence of native uncertainty quantification.

### GraphCast: GNN-based medium-range forecasting

GraphCast (Lam et al., 2023, "Learning Skillful Medium-Range Global Weather Forecasting," *Science*, 382(6677), 1416–1421) employs a GNN in an encode-process-decode configuration with 36.7 million parameters. The encoder maps 721 × 1,440 lat-lon grid points to a multi-mesh of 40,962 nodes derived from iteratively refined icosahedra (providing spatially homogeneous global coverage). The processor applies **16 unshared GNN layers** with learned message-passing for both local and long-range information propagation. The decoder maps back to the lat-lon grid, predicting residual updates for 227 variables (5 surface + 6 atmospheric × 37 pressure levels).

Trained on ERA5 (1979–2017) with autoregressive curriculum scheduling over approximately 4 weeks on 32 Cloud TPU v4 devices, GraphCast outperformed HRES on **over 90% of 1,380 verification targets**, rising to 99.7% in the troposphere. It generates 10-day forecasts in **under 1 minute on a single TPU v4** — a speedup of roughly four orders of magnitude over NWP. GraphCast accurately predicted Hurricane Lee's Nova Scotia landfall 9 days in advance, 3 days earlier than traditional NWP. ECMWF runs GraphCast experimentally, and NOAA has explored fine-tuning on GDAS data. Open-source code and weights are available on GitHub.

### FourCastNet: Fourier neural operators for weather

FourCastNet (Pathak et al., 2022, "FourCastNet: A Global Data-Driven High-Resolution Weather Model Using Adaptive Fourier Neural Operators," *arXiv:2202.11214*) was the first competitive AI weather model, employing a Vision Transformer backbone with **Adaptive Fourier Neural Operator (AFNO) attention** that replaces standard self-attention with learned spectral filtering in the Fourier domain, achieving O(N log N) complexity. Trained on ERA5 at 0.25° resolution, it generates week-long forecasts in under 2 seconds — **80,000× faster** than NWP. While later models surpassed FourCastNet's medium-range accuracy, it established the feasibility of the approach and influenced subsequent architectures. FourCastNetv2 (Bonev et al., 2023) adopted Spherical Fourier Neural Operators for improved performance on the sphere.

### ClimaX: a foundation model designed for transfer learning

ClimaX (Nguyen et al., 2023, "ClimaX: A Foundation Model for Weather and Climate," *ICML*, PMLR 202) is the first true foundation model for weather and climate, distinguished by its **variable tokenisation** (each input variable independently tokenised) and **variable aggregation** (combining all variables for a given location into a unified representation), enabling heterogeneous data ingestion across datasets with different variables and resolutions. Pre-trained on **CMIP6 climate simulation data** via self-supervised learning, then fine-tuned on ERA5, ClimaX can be adapted for global forecasting, regional forecasting, sub-seasonal prediction, and climate projections using task-specific prediction heads. Operating at lower resolution (1.4–5.6°) than Pangu-Weather or GraphCast, it prioritises flexibility and transferability over peak deterministic performance. It is open-source on GitHub.

### FuXi: cascaded architecture for extended-range forecasting

FuXi (Chen et al., 2023, "FuXi: A Cascade Machine Learning Forecasting System for 15-day Global Weather Forecast," *npj Climate and Atmospheric Science*, 6, 190) employs a **Swin Transformer V2 backbone with U-Net-style architecture** (U-Transformer) processing inputs through Cube Embedding (3D convolution reducing dimensions from 2×70×721×1440 to C×180×360). The distinctive feature is a **cascaded training strategy** with three separate models — FuXi-Short (0–5 days), FuXi-Medium (5–10 days), and FuXi-Long (10–15 days) — each fine-tuned from a pre-trained base, reducing error accumulation compared to single-model approaches. FuXi was the first ML model to achieve **15-day forecast performance comparable to the ECMWF Ensemble Mean**, extending skillful Z500 forecasts from 9.25 days (HRES) to 10.5 days and T2M forecasts from 10 to 14.5 days. Extensions include FuXi-ENS (2025, *Science Advances*) for ensemble forecasting via variational autoencoders and FuXi-Extreme for diffusion-based enhancement of fine-scale surface details.

### GenCast: probabilistic ensemble forecasting via diffusion

GenCast (Price et al., 2024, "Probabilistic Weather Forecasting with Machine Learning," *Nature*) represents a fundamental advance by providing **native probabilistic forecasts** through conditional diffusion modelling — addressing the most significant limitation of all preceding AI weather models. The architecture combines a denoising diffusion framework (iteratively refining noisy atmospheric states conditioned on the two most recent weather states) with a sparse transformer operating on a refined icosahedral mesh. Running 50+ ensemble members produces calibrated forecast distributions.

GenCast demonstrated **greater skill than the ECMWF ENS** (the world's best operational ensemble system) on 97.4% of 1,320 verification targets — rising to 99.8% at lead times beyond 36 hours. It produces well-calibrated rank histograms, superior CRPS scores, and better predictions of extreme weather events (heat/cold waves, high winds), tropical cyclone tracks, and wind power production. Each 15-day forecast takes approximately 8 minutes on a single Google Cloud TPU v5, with ensemble members generatable in parallel.

### Aurora: the largest and most flexible foundation model

Aurora (Bodnar et al., 2025, "A Foundation Model for the Earth System," *Nature*, 641, 1180–1187) is the largest weather foundation model at **1.3 billion parameters**, employing a flexible 3D Swin Transformer with U-Net backbone and Perceiver-based encoders/decoders that translate heterogeneous input data (different variables, resolutions, pressure levels) into a standardised latent representation via cross-attention. Pre-trained on over 1 million hours of diverse geophysical data — described as the largest collection of atmospheric data ever assembled for AI weather training — Aurora operates at **0.1° resolution (~11 km)**, matching ECMWF HRES native resolution.

Aurora outperforms GraphCast on **94% of verification targets** and produces 10-day weather forecasts with approximately 5,000× computational speed-up over IFS. It has demonstrated successful prediction of Storm Ciarán's rapid intensification and an Iraqi sandstorm one day in advance. Four specialised fine-tuned versions address medium-resolution weather, high-resolution weather, air pollution (PM10, NO₂), and ocean wave prediction. Lehmann et al. (2025) showed that lightweight decoders trained on Aurora's frozen latent representations can predict new hydrological variables not seen during pre-training, with 50% less training time and 35% less memory.

### Comparison with traditional NWP and limitations for local forecasting

AI weather models now surpass NWP on the majority of standard verification metrics for deterministic global forecasting (Pangu-Weather, GraphCast, Aurora), ensemble probabilistic forecasting (GenCast, FuXi-ENS), tropical cyclone track prediction, and computational efficiency (four to five orders of magnitude faster). However, NWP retains critical advantages for **record-breaking extremes** — a 2025 study (arXiv:2508.15724) found that physics-based models outperform AI forecasts of events outside the training distribution, since AI models trained on 1979–2017 data cannot extrapolate beyond historical observations. NWP also remains superior for **tropical cyclone intensity** prediction (AI models consistently underpredict maximum winds due to regression-toward-mean bias; Shi et al., 2025, *JGR*), **precipitation intensity** (spatial smoothing attenuates extreme rainfall peaks; Radford et al., 2025, *Weather and Forecasting*), and **physical consistency** (AI models do not enforce conservation laws, energy balances, or dynamical constraints).

All AI weather models share a critical dependency: they require **NWP-based analyses** (ERA5 or HRES-fc0) as initial conditions and cannot function without the traditional data assimilation system. For local-scale forecasting, the resolution gap remains significant — most global AI models operate at 0.25° (~28 km), with Aurora reaching 0.1° (~11 km), while many local applications require sub-kilometre resolution. Topographic effects, urban heat islands, coastal circulations, convection, and boundary-layer turbulence are poorly resolved. Emerging approaches to bridging this gap include statistical post-processing of foundation model output (Bremnes et al., 2024), stretched-grid variants at 6 km resolution over targeted regions (2025, *npj Climate and Atmospheric Science*), and IBM/NASA's Prithvi-WxC model (2.3 billion parameters, MERRA-2 trained) capable of 12× downscaling with fine-tuning.

## 6. Multi-task learning in weather prediction

### Theoretical motivation and architectural approaches

Multi-task learning (MTL) in weather prediction exploits the fundamental physical coupling between atmospheric variables. Temperature, humidity, pressure, and wind are governed by the same thermodynamic and dynamical equations — the Clausius-Clapeyron relation links temperature and saturation vapour pressure, the hydrostatic equation relates pressure and temperature profiles, and the equations of motion couple wind fields to pressure gradients. A shared encoder can learn general atmospheric state representations useful across prediction tasks, reducing redundancy and providing **implicit regularisation** that prevents overfitting on individual variables (Caruana, 1998).

Two primary architectural paradigms exist. **Hard parameter sharing** uses a single shared encoder with task-specific decoder heads — the dominant approach in current weather models. FengWu (Chen et al., 2023, "FengWu: Pushing the Skillful Global Medium-range Weather Forecast beyond 10 Days Lead," *arXiv:2304.02948*; published in *Communications Earth & Environment*, 2025) exemplifies a more sophisticated variant: an "encode-fuse-decode" structure with **modality-specific encoders** for each variable group (upper-air variables at different pressure levels, surface variables), a **cross-modal fusion Transformer** to capture inter-variable interactions, and modality-specific decoders. This architecture extends skillful Z500 forecasts beyond 10.75 days, outperforming HRES, Pangu-Weather, and GraphCast on multiple metrics. **Soft parameter sharing** maintains separate networks per task with learned connections between them. Cross-stitch networks (Misra et al., 2016, "Cross-stitch Networks for Multi-task Learning," *CVPR*) combine activations from task-specific networks via learned linear combinations at each layer, allowing the degree of sharing to be optimised end-to-end.

Chu et al. (2021, "Visual Weather Property Prediction by Multi-Task Learning and Two-Dimensional RNNs," *Atmosphere*, 12(5), 584) jointly predicted temperature, humidity, visibility, and wind speed from outdoor webcam images using a shared CNN backbone with 2D RNN temporal modelling, demonstrating that MTL outperformed single-task models for all four properties. Qiu et al. (2017, "A Short-Term Rainfall Prediction Model Using Multi-task Convolutional Neural Networks," *IEEE ICDM*, 395–404) pioneered MTL for multi-site rainfall prediction, treating each station's prediction as a separate task sharing convolutional feature extraction.

### Loss function design for heterogeneous targets

The combination of regression and classification losses within a single weather prediction model — for instance, predicting continuous temperature alongside binary precipitation occurrence — requires principled loss weighting. Kendall et al. (2018, "Multi-Task Learning Using Uncertainty to Weigh Losses for Scene Geometry and Semantics," *CVPR*, 7482–7491) derived task weights from **homoscedastic aleatoric uncertainty**: L(W, σ₁, σ₂) = (1/2σ₁²)L₁(W) + (1/2σ₂²)L₂(W) + log(σ₁) + log(σ₂), where σᵢ are learned noise parameters per task. Noisier tasks automatically receive smaller weights, and the logarithmic regularisation prevents unbounded growth of the noise parameters.

FengWu directly adopts this framework, extending it to **pixel-wise and channel-wise uncertainty**: each spatial location (w, h) and variable channel (c) has its own learned σ_{c,w,h}, making the loss region-adaptive — critical because prediction difficulty varies geographically between poles and tropics, land and ocean. Höhlein et al. (2023, "Physics-Constrained Multi-Task Postprocessing," *Artificial Intelligence for the Earth Systems*, 2(4)) applied Kendall-style uncertainty weighting to jointly post-process NWP temperature and humidity at 131 Swiss weather stations, noting that the approach is "fairly intuitive because tasks have different scales (due to different units) and uncertainties."

### Uncertainty quantification in multi-task weather models

Weather prediction inherently involves two types of uncertainty. **Aleatoric uncertainty** arises from irreducible stochasticity in atmospheric dynamics (chaotic sensitivity to initial conditions, sensor noise) and dominates at short forecast lead times. **Epistemic uncertainty** reflects model limitations and limited training data, becoming dominant at longer lead times as accumulated model errors grow (Hüllermeier and Waegeman, 2021, "Aleatoric and Epistemic Uncertainty in Machine Learning," *Machine Learning*, 110, 457–506).

Mansfield and Watson-Parris (2025, *arXiv:2511.23448*) demonstrated using Bayesian Neural Networks on the Lorenz 1996 system that "on weather timescales, aleatoric uncertainty dominates, underscoring the value of stochastic parameterisations" while "on longer, climate timescales and under changing forcings, accounting for both types is necessary." A hybrid Bayesian deep learning framework (2025, *arXiv:2511.14218*) explicitly decomposed uncertainty into epistemic (via variational inference) and aleatoric (via physics-informed stochastic perturbations) components, achieving **2.94× faster training** than GenCast with 19.52× speedup for 15-day ensemble forecasting. Haynes et al. (2023, *arXiv:2309.13207*) applied evidential deep learning to winter precipitation type classification and surface layer flux regression, rivalling ensemble performance with single-model efficiency.

### Gradient conflict and optimisation challenges

When training on multiple weather variables simultaneously, gradient conflicts arise when the optimal update direction for one variable opposes that of another. GradNorm (Chen et al., 2018, "GradNorm: Gradient Normalization for Adaptive Loss Balancing in Deep Multitask Networks," *ICML*, 794–803) dynamically tunes loss weights so that gradient norms across tasks are balanced, using a single hyperparameter α to control the target training rate ratio. PCGrad (Yu et al., 2020, "Gradient Surgery for Multi-Task Learning," *NeurIPS*) takes a geometric approach: when two task gradients conflict (negative cosine similarity), each is projected onto the normal plane of the other, removing the conflicting component while preserving the non-conflicting portion.

In weather prediction, the scale of the multi-task problem — hundreds of output variables spanning temperature (Kelvin), specific humidity (kg/kg), and pressure (Pa) — makes per-task gradient computation in PCGrad or GradNorm extremely expensive. Zhang et al. (2024) identified a "tragic triad" where gradient conflict is most harmful when conflicting directions, high positive curvature, and large magnitude differences co-occur — conditions frequently met in weather prediction due to vastly different variable scales. In practice, **uncertainty-weighted losses (Kendall-style) scale better** to high-dimensional multi-task settings than explicit gradient manipulation, as demonstrated by FengWu's successful handling of 189 output channels.

## Conclusion

The trajectory from Hochreiter and Schmidhuber's LSTM in 1997 to Aurora's 1.3-billion-parameter foundation model in 2025 represents one of the most consequential technological progressions in atmospheric science. Several key insights emerge from this review.

First, **architectural innovation has shifted from recurrence to attention to scale**. The critical breakthroughs — LSTM's gated memory, the Transformer's parallel self-attention, PatchTST's patch tokenisation, iTransformer's inverted dimensions — each resolved a specific bottleneck rather than offering uniformly superior alternatives. LSTMs remain competitive for short-horizon, small-dataset, and physically interpretable applications. Transformers dominate for long-sequence multivariate forecasting. The optimal architecture depends on the timescale and dimensionality of the target problem.

Second, **foundation models have fundamentally changed the cost-benefit calculus** of weather prediction. Systems like GenCast and Aurora produce forecasts superior to operational NWP at a fraction of the computational cost, though their dependence on NWP-produced initial conditions means they complement rather than replace physics-based systems. The remaining performance gaps — extreme events, precipitation intensity, tropical cyclone structure, physical consistency — point to the limitations of learning exclusively from historical data.

Third, **the local-scale forecasting problem remains substantially unsolved**. Despite impressive global-scale results, bridging the gap from 0.1–0.25° grid resolution to the point-scale predictions needed at individual observatories, weather stations, or renewable energy installations requires either learned downscaling, statistical post-processing, or fine-tuning approaches that are still in early development. The emerging paradigm of foundation model pre-training followed by lightweight task-specific fine-tuning — demonstrated by ClimaX and Aurora — offers the most promising path toward democratising high-quality local forecasts, particularly in data-sparse regions.

Fourth, **multi-task learning and uncertainty quantification are converging**. The recognition that weather variables are physically coupled, that prediction confidence varies spatially and across variables, and that both aleatoric and epistemic uncertainty must be quantified is driving architectures toward integrated probabilistic multi-task frameworks. FengWu's region-adaptive uncertainty-weighted loss and GenCast's diffusion-based ensemble generation represent early exemplars of this convergence, but systematic investigation of gradient conflict resolution, task relationship modelling, and calibrated uncertainty across hundreds of atmospheric variables remains a fertile area for future research.
