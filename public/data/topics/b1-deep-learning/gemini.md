# **Deep Learning Architectures for Meteorological Time Series Forecasting: A Comprehensive Literature Review**

The intersection of atmospheric sciences and artificial intelligence has precipitated a profound paradigm shift in meteorological forecasting. Historically, weather prediction and climate modeling have been overwhelmingly dominated by numerical weather prediction (NWP) systems, which rely on solving complex, non-linear partial differential equations representing fluid dynamics and atmospheric thermodynamics on supercomputing clusters. While highly accurate, NWP models demand immense computational resources, exhibit inherent limitations related to sub-grid parameterization, and struggle to assimilate diverse, non-traditional data streams. Parallel to physical models, classical statistical approaches such as Autoregressive Integrated Moving Average (ARIMA) and exponential smoothing have been widely utilized for localized time series forecasting.1 However, these traditional parametric models struggle to encapsulate the highly non-linear, chaotic, and multi-dimensional spatial-temporal dynamics inherent to meteorological phenomena over extended horizons.

The exponential proliferation of meteorological data—from high-resolution satellite imagery and ground-based Doppler radar to dense, global networks of automated weather stations—has catalyzed the adoption of deep learning architectures. Deep neural networks uniquely possess the capacity to automatically extract hierarchical features from complex, high-dimensional data, bypassing the need for explicit physical parameterization or manual feature engineering.3 This literature review provides an exhaustive technical analysis of the evolution, architectural nuances, and practical application of deep learning models in meteorological time series forecasting, tracing the trajectory from foundational Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs) to contemporary, planetary-scale Weather Foundation Models (WFMs).

## **1\. The Evolutionary Trajectory of Deep Learning for Time Series**

The evolution of deep learning for time series forecasting represents a continuous mathematical struggle to effectively capture temporal dependencies across varying horizons while simultaneously mitigating computational bottlenecks. The trajectory can be conceptualized in distinct generational leaps, each defined by a key structural breakthrough that resolved the limitations of its predecessor.

Early applications of artificial neural networks to time series relied on simple Multi-Layer Perceptrons (MLPs) and foundational Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs).3 Simple RNNs, popularized in the late 1980s and early 1990s, introduced the concept of hidden states to maintain a memory of preceding inputs, theoretically allowing the network to process sequences of varying lengths. However, they were fundamentally crippled by the vanishing and exploding gradient problems during backpropagation through time (BPTT). This mathematical limitation rendered them incapable of learning long-term dependencies, which are critical in meteorology for capturing seasonal cycles, annual oscillations, and slowly evolving climate phenomena.

The introduction of the Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) network in 1997, followed by the Gated Recurrent Unit (GRU) in 2014, provided the first major architectural breakthrough in sequential modeling.6 By introducing sophisticated internal gating mechanisms, these networks could actively regulate the flow of information, maintaining stable gradients over extended sequences. LSTMs and GRUs rapidly became the industry standard for sequential meteorological data, dominating tasks ranging from solar irradiance forecasting to temperature and precipitation prediction over the subsequent two decades.

Despite their empirical success, RNN-based architectures suffered from a strict sequential processing requirement, precluding parallelization and limiting their scalability on modern hardware accelerators. Furthermore, their performance consistently degraded as the context window expanded into thousands of time steps. The integration of Attention mechanisms, initially proposed for machine translation around 2014, provided a partial solution, allowing recurrent networks to selectively weigh the importance of different historical time steps rather than relying solely on the final hidden state.7

The definitive architectural leap occurred with the introduction of the Transformer model in 2017\. Transformers replaced recurrence entirely with self-attention mechanisms, enabling massive parallelization and global receptive fields.8 While originally designed for natural language processing, adapting Transformers for continuous, numerical time series data spurred a distinct lineage of research.10 Between 2019 and 2022, innovations such as the Temporal Fusion Transformer (TFT) introduced specialized multi-horizon forecasting capabilities, while architectures like Informer, Autoformer, and FEDformer tackled the quadratic complexity of canonical self-attention to accommodate extremely long meteorological sequences.10 Recent advances in 2023 and 2024 have fundamentally rethought the tokenization of time series, leading to models like PatchTST and iTransformer, which treat patches of time or entire variables as discrete tokens to better capture multivariate atmospheric physics.13

The most recent and profound evolution—emerging predominantly between 2023 and 2025—is the advent of Weather Foundation Models (WFMs). Diverging from models trained on localized station data, these architectures are trained on vast global reanalysis datasets like ERA5.15 Operating at grid resolutions comparable to operational NWP systems, models such as Pangu-Weather, GraphCast, and Aurora have demonstrated the unprecedented ability to match or exceed physics-based models in predicting complex global atmospheric dynamics, signaling a historic transition from deductive, physics-driven forecasting to inductive, data-driven emulation.18

## **2\. LSTM and Variants in Weather Forecasting**

The Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) network remains a highly robust and foundational architecture for meteorological forecasting due to its precise control over temporal memory. Unlike standard RNNs, the LSTM cell contains a core cell state that acts as an informational highway, regulated by three distinct gates: the forget gate, the input gate, and the output gate. The forget gate dictates the proportion of the previous cell state to discard, the input gate determines what new temporal information should be stored, and the output gate controls the emission of the hidden state for the current time step.20

This specific gating logic is exceptionally well-suited for weather data, which typically exhibits superimposed cycles—such as diurnal and annual temperature fluctuations—alongside short-term stochastic variations. The LSTM can learn to retain long-term seasonal trends within the core cell state while simultaneously updating short-term volatile phenomena, such as a sudden drop in barometric pressure, via the input gate.

### **Structural Depth: Stacked vs. Single-Layer LSTM**

The architectural depth of an LSTM network—determined by the number of stacked recurrent layers—represents a critical hyperparameter in meteorological modeling that dictates the model's capacity to understand complex atmospheric interactions. A single-layer LSTM processes the raw sequence directly, making it computationally efficient and highly suitable for simple, univariate predictions or scenarios with limited historical data.21 However, atmospheric conditions are driven by highly complex, non-linear thermodynamic interactions that a single layer often fails to fully abstract.

Stacked LSTMs involve placing multiple LSTM layers sequentially, where the hidden state output of one layer serves as the input sequence to the subsequent higher layer.22 This hierarchical structure allows the network to learn increasingly abstract and complex temporal representations at varying timescales. Comparative studies continuously demonstrate that stacked architectures generally outperform single-layer networks in capturing complex weather phenomena. For instance, in predictive modeling for photovoltaic power generation based on dilated causal convolutions, increasing the depth to two or three stacked LSTM layers yields significant reductions in prediction error by capturing the intricate, non-linear correlations between historical cloud cover, temperature, and resulting irradiance.23

However, the benefits of depth are strictly bounded by a capacity-robustness trade-off. Empirical evidence suggests that as the number of stacked layers increases beyond optimal levels—typically stabilizing at three to four layers—models become highly susceptible to overfitting, particularly given the noise and measurement artifacts inherent in meteorological sensor data.24 Furthermore, excessive depth exacerbates computational latency during both training and inference, acting as a critical deterrent for operational real-time nowcasting systems.

### **Bidirectional LSTMs and Attention Integration**

Bidirectional LSTMs (Bi-LSTMs) extend the temporal modeling capacity by processing sequences simultaneously in both forward and backward directions. This dual processing stream allows the network to incorporate both historical and future contexts into the representation of a specific temporal state.22 In a strict, operational real-time forecasting environment, the pure predictive application of Bi-LSTMs is theoretically constrained, as future meteorological states are fundamentally unobservable. Consequently, in a regular Bi-LSTM forecasting scenario, the future context is either omitted or masked, which limits the architecture's inherent advantages.22 However, Bi-LSTMs are heavily and successfully utilized in meteorological data imputation, reanalysis modeling, and post-processing tasks where the entire historical time series sequence is available for smoothing, error correction, and filling spatial-temporal gaps.25

To further address the degradation of sequential memory over extended forecasting horizons, LSTMs are frequently augmented with attention mechanisms. An attention-enhanced LSTM dynamically calculates alignment scores between the current forecasting step and all past hidden states, generating a context vector that weights the historical inputs by their immediate relevance.26 This is particularly critical in weather forecasting, where sudden phase shifts—such as the rapid onset of a convective storm or a frontal passage—render recent specific observations vastly more important than older, steady-state climatic data.

### **Specific Meteorological Applications, Datasets, and Performance**

The versatility of the LSTM and its variants is evidenced by their ubiquitous application across diverse meteorological sub-domains, heavily supported by specific empirical studies.

In the domain of temperature and multi-variable atmospheric prediction, researchers have extensively utilized the Jena Climate dataset, a benchmark corpus comprising 14 meteorological parameters recorded at high frequencies. Studies applying hybrid CNN-LSTM and specialized LSTM configurations to the Jena dataset have demonstrated substantial superiority over traditional ARIMA models and baseline Random Forests, specifically in capturing the complex interplay between pressure, relative humidity, and ambient temperature.2

Rainfall and precipitation nowcasting represents a significantly more volatile challenge due to the intermittent nature of rain events. Foundational papers, such as the original ConvLSTM research by Shi et al. (2015), revolutionized this space by applying modified LSTMs to radar echo datasets.28 Subsequent studies have utilized global satellite datasets, such as the NASA Global Precipitation Measurement (GPM) corpus, utilizing 30-minute interval sequences. Models integrating LSTM frameworks with spatial architectures have successfully predicted precipitation trajectories up to 180 minutes in advance, demonstrating that increased depth and dense historical sequences exponentially improve the structural quality of the forecast.29

The prediction of Total Cloud Cover (TCC) is critical for aviation, agriculture, and solar energy generation. Specific papers have applied LSTM variants to datasets like CloudCast, a comprehensive European dataset containing 70,080 high-resolution images categorized into 11 distinct cloud types at 15-minute intervals.30 By employing region-based Otsu's segmentation methods combined with time-series feature extraction, researchers have trained Bi-directional and CNN-LSTM models to forecast future TCC. These architectures achieved impressive short-term accuracy, reporting average Root Mean Square Error (RMSE) scores of 0.0543 for multi-step daytime cloud cover forecasting.31

In the highly specialized domain of astronomical "seeing" prediction, LSTMs are utilized to forecast atmospheric optical turbulence. Telescopic observations are heavily degraded by wavefront distortions caused by the turbulent mixing of air masses with varying refractive indices in the planetary boundary layer.32 By training LSTMs on multi-layer meteorological data—including wind speed gradients, air temperature, and pressure fluctuations—from Automated Weather Stations (AWS), models can predict the Fried parameter with remarkable precision. Landmark studies conducted at extreme, unattended observatory sites, such as Dome A in Antarctica, report that LSTM models, when coupled with Gaussian Process Regression, can predict seeing conditions 20 minutes in the future with an RMSE of merely 0.12 arcseconds, significantly outperforming classical persistence models and enabling dynamic optimization of telescope scheduling.34

## **3\. Transformer-Based Models for Weather and Climate**

While RNNs sequentially process data, the Transformer architecture leverages the self-attention mechanism to process entire sequences simultaneously, dynamically comparing every time step against every other time step to calculate comprehensive correlation matrices.8 This complete parallelization fundamentally resolves the vanishing gradient problem over extended sequences, allowing for greatly expanded look-back windows. However, the canonical Transformer exhibits a quadratic time and memory complexity—![][image1]—with respect to sequence length ![][image2]. This computational bottleneck historically constrained its direct application to high-frequency or long-horizon meteorological data, precipitating the development of specialized time-series Transformers.12

### **The Temporal Fusion Transformer (TFT)**

The Temporal Fusion Transformer (TFT) represents a highly specialized adaptation designed explicitly for multi-horizon time series forecasting. Meteorological forecasting rarely occurs in a vacuum; it requires the integration of diverse, heterogeneous data types. The TFT architecture is uniquely capable of harmonizing static covariates (such as station altitude, soil type, and topography), past-observed dynamic inputs (historical temperature and pressure readings), and a priori known future inputs (time of day, day of the week, or scheduled events).7

The TFT employs a sophisticated variable selection network that dynamically weighs the relevance of specific meteorological predictors at each time step, applying specialized Gated Residual Networks to suppress extraneous noise and focus on dominant signals.35 Furthermore, rather than emitting a deterministic point forecast, the TFT utilizes quantile loss functions to generate rigorous prediction intervals. This provides critical probabilistic bounds that reflect the inherent uncertainty in atmospheric predictions, a vital feature for operational meteorologists assessing risk.36 Interpretability is natively preserved through its masked multi-head attention; researchers can visualize attention weights to understand exactly which historical storms or specific variables drove a particular long-term prediction.37

### **Informer, Autoformer, and FEDformer**

To tackle the quadratic complexity bottleneck of the canonical Transformer, a lineage of highly efficient architectures emerged, specifically tailored for long-sequence time series forecasting (LSTF).

The Informer architecture introduced the ProbSparse self-attention mechanism. By recognizing that the self-attention probability distribution is typically sparse in long sequences, ProbSparse selectively identifies the most dominant queries based on a Kullback-Leibler divergence metric. This mathematical optimization effectively reduces the time and memory complexity from ![][image1] to ![][image3].12 The Informer also utilizes a generative-style multi-step decoder, enabling the prediction of extremely long meteorological sequences in a single forward operation, drastically reducing the inference latency associated with step-by-step autoregressive decoding.

The Autoformer advanced this paradigm by embedding classical time series decomposition directly within the deep learning architecture. Recognizing that weather data possesses strong seasonal, diurnal, and underlying trend components, the Autoformer utilizes an inner decomposition block that iteratively separates the hidden states into trend-cyclical and seasonal components.10 It replaces standard point-wise attention with an Auto-Correlation mechanism, which calculates sequence-level connections based on stochastic process theory. This design demonstrates superior performance and stability in capturing the rhythmic oscillations of diurnal temperature drops and continuous pressure systems.40

The FEDformer (Frequency Enhanced Decomposed Transformer) pushes structural efficiency further by operating entirely in the frequency domain. Utilizing discrete Fourier and Wavelet transforms, the FEDformer maps the time series representation into a frequency space, performing attention operations on a sparse set of selected frequency modes before mapping the representation back to the temporal domain.10 Because atmospheric cycles (such as the Madden-Julian Oscillation or planetary Rossby waves) are inherently harmonic, this frequency-domain attention is exceptionally adept at capturing long-term meteorological patterns while maintaining linear computational complexity.

### **Recent Advances: PatchTST and iTransformer**

Despite the success of Informer and Autoformer, subsequent empirical benchmarks revealed instances where simple linear models occasionally outperformed sophisticated Transformers on specific datasets, sparking a debate regarding temporal information loss in self-attention mechanisms.11 The core issue stemmed from point-wise tokenization; embedding single time steps individually stripped the data of local semantic meaning.

PatchTST resolved this by dividing univariate time series into distinct, sub-sequence patches. By treating a multi-step patch (e.g., a 24-hour block) as a single token, PatchTST significantly increased the local semantic richness while simultaneously dropping the computational complexity to ![][image4], where ![][image5] is the patch length. This channel-independent architecture allowed the model to leverage exponentially longer look-back windows to extract deep historical context, proving exceptionally dominant in univariate forecasting tasks like solar irradiance and temperature prediction.41

The iTransformer introduced an even more radical "architectural inversion" to address the complexities of multivariate forecasting. Traditional multivariate Transformers embed time steps across multiple variables into singular tokens, using attention to capture temporal dynamics. The iTransformer explicitly argues that meteorological variables (e.g., wind speed vs. specific humidity) possess completely different physical meanings, scales, and distributions. Therefore, it applies an inverted structure: the entire historical sequence of a single meteorological variable is embedded as one singular token.14

Data indicates a profound structural shift in how tokenization dictates model capabilities. In the iTransformer, the self-attention mechanism is applied *across* the variable dimension rather than the temporal dimension. This allows the model to calculate the profound physical correlations between disparate variables (e.g., the complex non-linear relationship between pressure drops, cloud cover, and wind speed), while utilizing the Feed-Forward Network to exclusively model the temporal dynamics of each individual variate. This strategy has demonstrated state-of-the-art performance in capturing complex multivariate dependencies in highly volatile atmospheric datasets, effectively resolving the limitations of temporal-only attention.13

### **Comparison: When Transformers Outperform LSTMs**

The decision to deploy a Transformer versus an LSTM hinges strictly on the data environment and the specific forecasting objective. Transformers definitively outperform LSTMs in Long-Term Time Series Forecasting (LTSF) tasks. Architectures like PatchTST and iTransformer excel when the look-back window extends to hundreds or thousands of steps, as their parallel attention mechanisms do not suffer from the temporal degradation that plagues RNNs.45 They are also superior in highly rhythmic, seasonal data, where the global receptive field can identify distant periodic patterns.

Conversely, LSTMs and recurrent variants maintain superiority in short-term, immediate nowcasting, particularly when the data exhibits high stochastic volatility, irregular sampling, or severe missing values. LSTMs naturally process sequential flow and adapt well to step-by-step state changes, making them more resilient to sudden, chaotic short-term fluctuations where a Transformer's global attention might over-smooth the immediate anomaly.45 Furthermore, the computational overhead of training a multi-layer Transformer often cannot be justified for simple, univariate, short-horizon tasks, where a stacked LSTM provides comparable accuracy at a fraction of the computational cost.47

## **4\. Spatiotemporal Hybrid Architectures**

Meteorological forecasting is intrinsically spatiotemporal; atmospheric phenomena evolve simultaneously across geographic space and time. While recurrent networks and Transformers excel at temporal sequence modeling, they are inefficient at parsing complex, multidimensional spatial structures like radar echo maps, satellite imagery grids, or geopotential height fields. To address this, hybrid architectures seamlessly fuse spatial feature extractors with temporal sequence models.3

### **CNN-LSTM and ConvLSTM**

The CNN-LSTM architecture utilizes a sequential modular decoupling approach. A 2D or 3D Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) operates as a spatial encoder, digesting sequences of weather maps to extract high-level geographic feature vectors. These spatially compressed, flattened vectors are then fed into a standard LSTM to model the temporal evolution. This architecture is highly prevalent in applications where spatial feature extraction is paramount, such as visual weather property prediction or regional solar irradiance forecasting based on cloud cover imagery.29

The Convolutional LSTM (ConvLSTM), conversely, was a seminal architecture specifically formulated to integrate spatial awareness directly into the recurrent mechanism for precipitation nowcasting. In a standard LSTM, the input-to-state and state-to-state transitions are executed via fully connected matrix multiplications, which fundamentally destroy the 2D spatial topology of meteorological grids. The ConvLSTM, introduced by Shi et al. (2015), replaces these dense connections with convolution operations directly within the input and recurrent state gates.28 By passing spatial feature maps through the LSTM gating mechanism, the network explicitly maintains the geographic structure of cloud movements, squall lines, and rain bands. This drastically reduces the parameter count while vastly improving the spatiotemporal prediction of localized storm cell trajectories on radar datasets.30

### **Deep Residual and Inception-Inspired LSTMs**

As CNN spatial encoders grow deeper to extract more nuanced atmospheric features, they suffer from the gradient degradation problem, where accuracy saturates and degrades rapidly during training. The ResNet-LSTM hybrid mitigates this by integrating residual shortcut connections within the spatial extraction phase.51 By ensuring stable gradient flow, the network can utilize highly deep architectures (e.g., ResNet50) to extract intricate localized patterns from complex topographies before passing the representations to the LSTM. This configuration is highly effective for short-term load forecasting linked to micro-climate temperature predictions and has demonstrated superior performance in estimating PM2.5/PM10 pollutant concentrations from sequential imagery.52

The Inception-LSTM modifies the architecture by recognizing that weather features operate at vastly different spatial scales simultaneously. A massive low-pressure frontal system and a localized, violent convective updraft require entirely different receptive fields to be modeled accurately. Inspired by the GoogLeNet architecture, Inception-LSTM employs multiple convolutional filters of varying sizes (e.g., 1x1, 3x3, 5x5) simultaneously within the network or directly within the recurrent gates.54 The multi-scale feature maps are concatenated, enabling the network to track both macro-scale weather movements and micro-scale localized disturbances synchronously, proving highly effective in complex short-term predictions.55

### **Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) \+ Temporal Models**

While CNN-based hybrids require data structured on regular Euclidean grids (like pixels in an image or standard latitude/longitude grids), physical weather observation networks are highly irregular. Weather stations are scattered unevenly across mountainous terrain, and global atmospheric modeling must account for the continuous spherical geometry of the Earth. Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) process non-Euclidean data naturally by representing observation points as nodes and the physical or statistical relationships between them as edges.56

When paired with temporal models, GNNs achieve unparalleled spatial modeling for real-world meteorological networks. In a weather-centric GNN, a node represents a specific weather station carrying a temporal feature vector (temperature, humidity sequences), and the edges reflect physical distance, elevation changes, or modeled atmospheric fluid flow. Graph convolutional layers pass messages between connected nodes, allowing the network to natively learn how a rapid pressure drop at one station influences its geographic neighbors over time.56 Furthermore, in global modeling, advanced GNNs operate on multi-scale hierarchical meshes mapped directly to the Earth's sphere, entirely circumventing the severe geometric distortions and pole-singularities caused by flattening the globe into a 2D Euclidean grid.57

## **5\. Weather Foundation Models (2023-2025)**

The most transformative and widely publicized development in recent meteorological literature is the rapid emergence of global Weather Foundation Models (WFMs). Leveraging the vast, homogenous archives of the ECMWF's ERA5 reanalysis—a dataset integrating decades of global observations, satellite telemetry, and physical modeling into a consistent atmospheric record—these massive deep learning architectures operate at high resolutions (typically 0.25° or 0.1°) across the entire globe.15

### **Architectural Diversity and Performance**

WFMs employ varied architectural backbones to master the massive dimensionality of global atmospheric states, representing a divergence in how to optimally model planetary physics.

* **Pangu-Weather (Huawei):** Utilizes a 3D Earth-Specific Swin Transformer architecture. Instead of processing flattened 2D slices of the atmosphere, it processes 3D volumetric patches, effectively capturing the complex vertical thermodynamic coupling between varying atmospheric pressure levels.17 To minimize cumulative autoregressive errors, Pangu-Weather operates with hierarchical temporal aggregation, utilizing specialized, independently trained models for different forecast lead times (e.g., 1-hour, 3-hour, 6-hour, 24-hour step models).61  
* **GraphCast (Google DeepMind):** Relies on a sophisticated encode-process-decode Graph Neural Network mapped to an icosahedral mesh of the Earth. This architecture explicitly preserves spherical geometry and ensures uniform spatial resolution at the poles and the equator, resolving a common failing point for standard grid-based convolutional models.60  
* **FourCastNet (NVIDIA):** Introduces the Adaptive Fourier Neural Operator (AFNO) to model the global atmosphere. By treating the atmospheric state as a continuous function and solving the token mixing heavily in the frequency domain, it achieves exceptional computational efficiency, operating thousands of times faster than traditional NWP models.16  
* **ClimaX:** Distinctive as the first true foundation model designed to scale across diverse tasks. Built on a Vision Transformer (ViT) backbone, ClimaX was pre-trained on highly heterogeneous datasets, including both ERA5 reanalysis and the output of CMIP6 climate models, allowing it to perform both weather forecasting and long-term climate projections.60  
* **FuXi:** Developed as a cascade machine learning forecasting system specifically optimized for the 15-day global weather forecast window, addressing the long-term degradation seen in earlier autoregressive models.60  
* **Aurora (Microsoft):** A uniquely versatile WFM built on 3D Swin Transformers. Unlike models trained solely on ERA5, Aurora was pre-trained on a massive, heterogeneous corpus of over one million hours of data encompassing six different weather and climate simulations (including CMCC, GFS, and IFS). This diverse pre-training grants the model profound generalization capabilities, allowing it to excel even when initial conditions vary.65  
* **GenCast (Google DeepMind):** Recognizing that deterministic models inherently produce blurry forecasts at longer lead times by optimizing for Mean Squared Error (MSE), GenCast implements a diffusion-based probabilistic generative architecture. Rather than predicting a single blurred mean state, it generates highly realistic, physically plausible ensemble members, providing vital probabilistic distributions for medium-range uncertainty forecasting.64

### **Comparisons to Traditional NWP Systems**

The performance of these models against the gold-standard NWP, the ECMWF Integrated Forecasting System High-Resolution (IFS-HRES), has catalyzed intense academic debate. Deterministic WFMs routinely report lower global RMSE and higher anomaly correlation coefficients for key macro-variables like 500-hPa geopotential height and 850-hPa temperature up to 10 days in advance.67 Their defining operational advantage is unprecedented computational latency; generating a high-resolution 10-day global forecast requires mere seconds on a single GPU, compared to hours on a supercomputing cluster for the IFS.68

Furthermore, extensive diagnostic evaluations demonstrate that models like Pangu-Weather have implicitly encoded sophisticated atmospheric physics purely from data. Perturbation experiments verify that the model correctly emulates the Matsuno-Gill response to tropical heating, realistic planetary wave propagation, and the spontaneous emergence of polar lows, proving it is not merely executing superficial pattern matching but genuinely simulating thermodynamic fluid mechanics.69

### **Limitations and Hyperlocal Fine-Tuning**

Despite extraordinary benchmark success, significant structural limitations persist. Deterministic WFMs inherently regress to the mean when uncertainty is high. Because they are optimized via MSE loss, they generate overly smooth, blurry predictions at extended lead times that severely underestimate the magnitude of extreme anomalies (e.g., peak wind speeds during an extratropical cyclone or localized precipitation maximums).18 In intensive case studies of extreme events, such as the 2021 Pacific Northwest heatwave, WFMs underperformed high-resolution NWP when aggregated spatially and temporally, frequently misjudging the peak intensity.70 Furthermore, their reliance on historical reanalysis renders them vulnerable to inheriting the structural biases of the training datasets, and their black-box nature complicates rigorous physical diagnostics.

Because WFMs are trained on global, relatively coarse data (0.25° equates to roughly 25-30km at the equator), applying them directly to regional, high-resolution tasks is suboptimal. The current frontier of WFM research involves bridging this gap via parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT) and dynamic downscaling.

Directly updating all billions of parameters in a WFM for a local task is computationally prohibitive and risks catastrophic forgetting of global dynamics. Methods leveraging Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) have been adopted to specialize global models for local domains. Architectures like WeatherPEFT inject task-adaptive dynamic prompts and lightweight trainable adapters into the frozen WFM backbone.71 This allows a global model like Aurora to be swiftly optimized for specific regional dynamics (e.g., the complex, data-scarce micro-climates of South Africa) using limited local station observations, dramatically improving regional RMSE without requiring supercomputer-level retraining.73 Alternatively, hybrid frameworks utilize AI models to generate the coarse global boundary conditions rapidly, which are then fed into a traditional physics-based regional model like the Weather Research and Forecasting (WRF) model for mathematically rigorous dynamic downscaling to the kilometer scale.74

## **6\. Multi-Task Learning in Weather Prediction**

Meteorological variables are governed by tightly coupled physical equations; temperature, atmospheric pressure, relative humidity, and precipitation do not evolve in isolation. Traditional single-task deep learning forecasting models fail to leverage these profound cross-variable correlations, often resulting in physical inconsistencies (e.g., a model predicting heavy precipitation without a corresponding drop in temperature or pressure). Multi-Task Learning (MTL) architectures address this deficiency by jointly optimizing a single neural network to predict multiple distinct but physically related meteorological parameters simultaneously.20

### **Shared Mechanisms and Network Design**

The foundation of an MTL weather architecture is the strategic division between shared feature extraction layers and task-specific output heads. A typical structure utilizes a powerful, high-capacity sequence backbone—such as a stacked LSTM-GRU configuration or a Transformer encoder—to process the multidimensional meteorological inputs.20 This backbone acts as a shared representation space where the network forces the latent features of temperature to inform and restrict the latent features of humidity, mimicking the natural physical constraints of the atmosphere.

Advanced frameworks employ asymmetric sharing mechanisms to mitigate the pervasive "correlation dependence" problem. In simple, fully shared models, strongly correlated parameters (like temperature and pressure) can dominate the loss space during backpropagation, suppressing the learning of weakly correlated but equally important variables. Asymmetric sharing introduces specifically engineered feature-sharing layers that permit controlled, artificial intervention in the parameter-sharing process. This architectural design allows specific sub-tasks to selectively draw relevant information from the shared pool while maintaining robust, independent feature representations, preventing one dominant weather variable from overriding the network's predictive capacity.20

### **Cross-Paradigm Forecasting and Loss Function Optimization**

MTL is particularly powerful when combining entirely different machine learning paradigms, such as simultaneous classification and regression tasks. In precipitation forecasting, determining the exact rainfall rate (regression) is notoriously difficult due to extreme zero-inflation (the vast majority of time steps record zero precipitation). Advanced MTL architectures tackle this by simultaneously training a binary or multi-class classification head (rain/no-rain, or weather type) alongside a continuous regression head (precipitation intensity or temperature).75

For example, in passive microwave precipitation retrieval studies, models concurrently execute rain/no-rain classification and rain rate regression. The shared feature extraction layers learn profound cloud-physics representations driven by the categorical labels, which actively stabilizes and improves the continuous accuracy of the regression outputs.75 Similar frameworks have been utilized for visual weather recognition, where CNN-RNN multi-task models simultaneously classify overarching weather conditions (e.g., clear, foggy) while regressing specific continuous properties like visibility distance and ambient temperature.78

The primary mathematical obstacle in implementing MTL for weather prediction is the design of the loss function. If the numerical scale of the Mean Squared Error (MSE) loss for atmospheric pressure drastically exceeds the cross-entropy loss for rain classification, the network will exclusively optimize for pressure. Manual tuning of relative loss weights is highly inefficient and brittle across dynamic atmospheric datasets. State-of-the-art architectures resolve this by treating homoscedastic uncertainty—the task-dependent, input-independent uncertainty—as a learnable noise parameter during training.

Furthermore, data indicates that by assuming a Laplace loss distribution rather than a standard Gaussian distribution, the network becomes vastly more robust to the extreme outliers commonly found in meteorological events.20 The model dynamically assigns lower weights to tasks with high inherent uncertainty during specific training phases. This mathematical formulation allows the gradients of all weather variables, regardless of their numerical scale or variance, to converge synchronously, maximizing the synergistic, regularizing benefits of the multi-task framework and ensuring physically coherent multi-variable forecasts.

## **7\. Conclusion**

The integration of deep learning into meteorological time series forecasting has catalyzed a monumental shift in predictive capability. The field has traversed rapidly from the strict sequential processing of localized LSTMs—which remain highly potent for specific, resource-constrained nowcasting tasks—to the massive, parallelized global reasoning of Transformer-based Weather Foundation Models. While traditional NWP systems maintain superiority in explicit physical consistency and the resolution of extreme, unprecedented anomalies, data-driven architectures have definitively proven their capacity to emulate complex atmospheric thermodynamics at a fraction of the computational cost.

The current frontier of research lies in addressing the deterministic limitations of these foundation models through generative probabilistic ensembles, and in bridging the gap between global emulation and hyperlocal forecasting via parameter-efficient fine-tuning and hybrid dynamic downscaling. As Multi-Task Learning structures mature and loss functions increasingly account for atmospheric uncertainty, the dichotomy between purely data-driven AI and traditional physical modeling will increasingly blur, culminating in seamlessly integrated, hyper-accurate Earth system emulators.
