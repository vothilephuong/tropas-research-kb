# **Rigorous Evaluation and Benchmarking Framework for Astronomical Meteorological Datasets**

The intersection of atmospheric sciences, astronomical site characterization, and machine learning necessitates highly curated, rigorously validated data streams. High-fidelity meteorological datasets are foundational not only for climatological analysis but also for optimizing astronomical observations, scheduling telescope operations, and training predictive models. The emergence of data-driven weather forecasting, propelled by deep learning and foundation models, has elevated the requirements for dataset curation, metadata standardization, and benchmarking protocols.1

Constructing a dataset that meets international standards and holds high scientific value requires a multidimensional evaluation framework. An observatory weather dataset must transcend basic meteorological logging; it must capture the complex thermodynamics, fluid dynamics, and optical properties of the atmosphere. To establish a new dataset as a benchmark-grade asset, it must be systematically evaluated across five critical dimensions: data quality, data richness, environmental representativeness, advanced time-series benchmarking methodology, and publishability criteria.

## **1\. Data Quality Metrics**

The utility of any environmental dataset is fundamentally constrained by its intrinsic quality. High-quality meteorological and astronomical datasets must adhere to stringent international standards, primarily those outlined by the World Meteorological Organization (WMO) and the International Organization for Standardization (ISO).3 The WMO Guide to Meteorological Instruments and Methods of Observation (WMO-No. 8\) serves as the authoritative reference for instrumentation, ensuring data uniformity and traceability across global observing systems.5

### **Completeness and Missing Data Topologies**

Completeness is conventionally quantified as the simple percentage of non-missing values per variable over a specified temporal domain. However, a singular aggregated percentage often masks the underlying topology of missing data, which is critical for time-series forecasting algorithms. Data gaps in observatory environments manifest in several distinct forms. Singular missing points frequently arise from transient sensor noise or brief telemetry packet loss. Sequential gaps are typically the result of catastrophic hardware failures, power outages, or extreme weather events that force sensor shutdown. Structured missingness occurs when instruments are only designed to operate under specific environmental conditions; for instance, optical seeing monitors like the Differential Image Motion Monitor (DIMM) cannot operate during daylight hours or through heavy cloud cover.6

For a dataset to be deemed rigorous, completeness must be evaluated at a highly granular level. High-tier climatological datasets report completeness exceeding 95% for fundamental meteorological variables such as temperature, barometric pressure, and wind vectors.8 When evaluating dataset quality, the mechanism utilized to handle missing values must be explicitly documented. Imputation techniques—whether simple linear interpolation, spline fitting, or advanced k-Nearest Neighbors (k-NN) algorithms—can artificially dampen the natural variance of the dataset. This artificial smoothing misleads autoregressive forecasting models, causing them to underestimate atmospheric volatility.

### **Temporal Coverage and Continuity**

Temporal coverage dictates the applicability of the dataset for capturing long-term climatic trends versus short-term synoptic weather events. Observational datasets spanning several decades provide the continuity required to filter out transient anomalies and establish robust climatological baselines. For example, the Global Historical Climatology Network (GHCN) integrates daily climate observations dating back to the 18th century, while the ERA5 atmospheric reanalysis archive spans from 1940 to the present.9

For astronomical observatory datasets, the total temporal duration must ideally encompass multiple phases of macroscopic climatic phenomena, such as the El Niño-Southern Oscillation (ENSO) or the North Atlantic Oscillation (NAO).11 Datasets limited to a few years risk being biased by a singular anomalous cycle, rendering models trained on them brittle when deployed in subsequent decades. Continuity refers to the uninterrupted sequence of these records. A highly continuous ten-year dataset with consistent hourly measurements provides exponentially more value to machine learning practitioners than a fragmented fifteen-year dataset marred by seasonal operational halts or funding shortfalls.

### **Sampling Rate Consistency**

The frequency of data acquisition defines the spectral bandwidth of atmospheric phenomena that can be resolved, governed strictly by the Nyquist-Shannon sampling theorem. The WMO-No. 8 guidelines recommend that raw instantaneous sensor samples be linearized and averaged over specific temporal intervals to produce standardized, comparable outputs.13 Variables such as atmospheric pressure, air temperature, and relative humidity are typically reported as 1-minute to 10-minute averages.13 Wind speed and direction, excluding transient peak gusts, are usually reported as 2-minute or 10-minute averages to smooth out highly localized micro-eddies.13

Astronomical site characterization, however, requires entirely different sampling paradigms. Datasets supporting adaptive optics and optical turbulence profiling require sub-second sampling rates to capture rapid atmospheric phase fluctuations. For example, DIMM instruments image stellar sources at frame rates of 100 Hz, while Multi-Aperture Scintillation Sensors (MASS) operate at 1 kHz.14 Consistency in these sampling rates across the lifespan of the dataset is critical. Altering the averaging window mid-record—such as shifting from a 1-minute to a 10-minute mean—introduces systemic, artificial biases into variance and volatility metrics, corrupting historical comparisons.

### **Sensor Accuracy and Calibration Standards**

Data fidelity relies heavily on hardware specifications and strict adherence to calibration schedules. Meteorological stations must comply with WMO and ISO 19289 siting classifications to prevent micro-environmental contamination.15 Sensors must be isolated from localized thermal radiation emitted by adjacent concrete structures, asphalt, or telescope domes, and anemometers must be elevated above the surface boundary layer to avoid flow distortion.16

Calibration standards must guarantee absolute traceability to primary standards. An exemplary benchmark for calibration is the Low Humidity and Temperature Profiling (LHATPRO) microwave radiometer utilized at the European Southern Observatory (ESO) Paranal site.17 This instrument profiles humidity using the 183 GHz water vapor emission line and temperature using the 51–58 GHz oxygen band.14 For absolute flux calibration, it utilizes an external target cooled to the boiling point of liquid nitrogen, ensuring accuracy down to precipitable water vapor (PWV) levels of 0.1 mm.17 Accuracy thresholds must be explicitly defined in the dataset metadata. Standard benchmarks require temperature readings to maintain an accuracy of ![][image1]C, wind speed to be accurate within ![][image2], and atmospheric seeing measurements to be accurate to better than 10% above 0.25 arcseconds.18 Comprehensive documentation of sensor drift over time and the exact dates of instrument recalibration or replacement is mandatory for any dataset aspiring to international publication standards.

### **Data Latency and Telemetry**

Latency—defined as the temporal delta between physical measurement, digital processing, and database availability—determines a dataset's viability for operational nowcasting and automated scheduling. Modern observatories function within highly automated, low-latency computational ecosystems. The Vera C. Rubin Observatory's Legacy Survey of Space and Time (LSST), which generates approximately 20 terabytes of data nightly, processes telemetry and issues transient event alerts within 60 seconds of image readout.19

Similarly, the WIGOS Data Quality Monitoring System (WDQMS) tracks the timeliness of observational data delivery globally, penalizing networks that suffer from transmission bottlenecks.22 Datasets must report maximum, minimum, and median latency metrics. Low latency is particularly critical if the meteorological data feeds directly into real-time automated telescope scheduling algorithms (flexible scheduling) or supports multimessenger astronomical alert networks, such as the rapid follow-up of gravitational wave events detected by LIGO and Virgo.23

### **Uptime and Availability Metrics**

Uptime translates directly to the operational reliability of the sensor array and the resilience of the surrounding infrastructure. Severe weather environments, such as the summit of Mauna Kea or the Antarctic plateau at Dome C, frequently subject hardware to catastrophic conditions, including rime icing, hurricane-force wind shear, and extreme thermal cycling down to ![][image3]C.25

The calculation of uptime must isolate genuine hardware or software failures from inherent environmental constraints. For example, optical seeing monitors inherently exhibit lower absolute uptime than barometers because they cannot operate through thick cloud cover, during precipitation, or during daylight hours.7 Therefore, the concept of "weather-adjusted uptime" is a necessary metric for astronomical datasets. This metric denotes the percentage of time an instrument was successfully recording data *during the specific environmental conditions in which it was physically capable of operating*. Reporting unadjusted uptime for optical sensors heavily penalizes sites with distinct monsoon or winter storm seasons, skewing cross-site comparisons.

## **2\. Data Richness and Feature Engineering**

The richness of a meteorological dataset is evaluated by its dimensionality, the presence of domain-specific specialized variables, and the integration of multiple distinct data streams. A dataset transcends standard climatology only when it successfully maps the complex vertical, thermodynamic, and optical characteristics of the atmosphere.

### **Diversity of Measured Variables**

Standard synoptic meteorological datasets capture basic scalar quantities at the surface boundary layer: near-surface air temperature, relative humidity, barometric pressure, wind vectors, and precipitation accumulation.27 An expert-level observatory dataset expands this manifold by measuring variables across multiple vertical strata to capture atmospheric gradients.

For example, the Paranal Astronomical Site Monitor (ASM) records wind speed and temperature at multiple heights along a 30-meter mast (e.g., 30m, 20m, 10m, and 2m above ground), allowing researchers to derive vertical thermal gradients and boundary layer shear stress.14 Additional specialized parameters, such as soil moisture, broadband aerosol optical depth (AOD), and shortwave/longwave radiative fluxes, further enrich the dataset's utility, enabling advanced thermodynamic modeling of the local environment.27

| Variable Category | Standard Meteorological Parameters | Advanced Observatory Parameters |
| :---- | :---- | :---- |
| **Thermodynamics** | Surface Temperature, Relative Humidity | Vertical Temp Gradients, Dew-point Depression, Soil Temp |
| **Kinematics** | 10m Wind Speed, Wind Direction | 30m Wind Vectors, Vertical Shear, High-Altitude Jet Stream |
| **Hydrology** | Precipitation Accumulation | Precipitable Water Vapor (PWV), Snow Depth, Ice Accumulation |
| **Radiation/Optics** | Solar Irradiance | Aerosol Optical Depth (AOD), Sky Luminance (SQM), Cloud Base Height |

### **Astronomy-Specific Parameters**

The defining characteristic of an astronomical weather dataset is the inclusion of optical turbulence and sky quality metrics. These parameters directly dictate the resolving power and signal-to-noise ratio of ground-based telescopes.

**Optical Turbulence and Seeing:** Atmospheric seeing is parameterized by the refractive index structure parameter, ![][image4], which quantifies the strength of optical turbulence caused by micro-thermal mixing in the atmosphere.7 Integrated seeing is typically measured using a DIMM, which observes the differential motion of a stellar image through two sub-apertures. Seeing is standardized as the Full Width at Half Maximum (FWHM) of a stellar image at a reference wavelength of 500 nm at zenith.14

Advanced datasets include vertical turbulence profiling using instruments like MASS or SLODAR (Slope Detection And Ranging).14 These instruments partition the ![][image4] profile into discrete altitude layers (e.g., 0.5 km to 16 km), distinguishing between boundary layer turbulence and high-altitude jet stream sheer.14 From these profiles, critical adaptive optics parameters are derived:

* **The Fried Parameter (![][image5]):** The spatial coherence length of the atmosphere, dictating the required spacing of deformable mirror actuators.30  
* **The Isoplanatic Angle (![][image6]):** The angular distance over which atmospheric turbulence is considered highly correlated.  
* **The Greenwood Time Constant (![][image7]):** The temporal coherence scale, dictating the necessary correction speed (bandwidth) for an adaptive optics system.30

**Night Sky Brightness (SQM):** Sky luminance is continuously monitored using Sky Quality Meters (SQM), capturing measurements in magnitudes per square arcsecond. The richness of SQM data is highly dependent on the isolation of astronomical versus atmospheric noise. High-tier datasets, such as those generated at the Roque de los Muchachos Observatory (ORM), acquire SQM data at high frequencies (e.g., every 20 seconds) but only activate when the sun is at least 10 degrees below the horizon.33 These datasets actively account for the sweeping passage of the Milky Way across the zenith, which can naturally decrease the magnitude by up to 0.5 mag.33 Furthermore, they map the effects of atmospheric phenomena, noting how thin cirrus clouds or Saharan dust intrusions (Calima) severely degrade magnitude by reflecting back terrestrial light pollution.33

**Precipitable Water Vapor (PWV):** PWV represents the total depth of water in an atmospheric column if it were entirely condensed. It is the primary source of atmospheric opacity in infrared and sub-millimeter astronomy.17 Top-tier datasets provide high-resolution continuous PWV monitoring utilizing microwave radiometers, or derive it indirectly by cross-referencing GPS hydrostatic delay metrics with satellite measurements.35

### **Derived and Engineered Features**

Raw telemetry is often less predictive than engineered thermodynamic and fluid dynamic features. Datasets that pre-compute derived variables provide immense value to machine learning practitioners by reducing computational overhead. Common derived features include the dew-point depression (a primary indicator of imminent fog formation or dome condensation) and the Richardson number (a dimensionless measure of atmospheric stability and turbulence generation). Advanced feature engineering might also include calculating the specific attenuation of distinct infrared wavebands based on concurrent PWV and aerosol optical depth readings, allowing for highly specific telescope scheduling.37

### **Spatial Coverage and Multi-Source Integration**

While many observatories rely on a single central meteorological mast, localized mountainous topography induces severe micro-meteorological variations. High-value datasets integrate spatial networks, combining data from multiple telescope domes distributed across a caldera or plateau to map localized wind channeling, thermal wakes, and localized cloud formation.39

Furthermore, ground truth must be contextualized within the broader macro-climate. The most rigorous datasets implement multi-source integration by fusing local in-situ sensor data with global gridded products. This involves the integration of satellite imagery—such as GOES geocolor data or MODIS cloud cover masks—and Numerical Weather Prediction (NWP) reanalysis models.41 The ERA5 reanalysis dataset, produced by the European Centre for Medium-Range Weather Forecasts (ECMWF), provides hourly estimates of atmospheric variables on a 31 km grid.9 Benchmarking local observatory data against ERA5 or the Global Data Assimilation System (GDAS) allows researchers to detect regional climate anomalies, identify long-term sensor drift, and create hybrid data-assimilation models.35

## **3\. Environmental Representativeness**

A dataset's generalizability is intrinsically linked to its environmental representativeness. If a meteorological dataset only spans optimal observing seasons or entirely excludes anomalous weather events, any predictive model trained upon it will suffer from catastrophic degradation when exposed to out-of-distribution atmospheric regimes.

### **Climate Zone and Microclimate Coverage**

Observatories are systematically situated in highly specific, extreme geographical topologies to maximize atmospheric transparency. These include the high-altitude, hyper-arid Atacama Desert (Paranal), isolated oceanic shield volcanoes (Mauna Kea), or high-relief volcanic calderas (Roque de los Muchachos).17 The dataset documentation must clearly delineate the broader macro-climatic zone (e.g., via the Köppen climate classification system) while extensively detailing the site's unique microclimate dynamics.

For instance, Mauna Kea's atmospheric profile is heavily dominated by the Northeast Pacific anticyclone, which drives oceanic trade winds and creates a persistent thermal inversion layer at approximately 2000 meters.44 This inversion acts as a physical barrier, trapping maritime moisture and aerosols below the summit, resulting in the exceptionally dry and stable air required for astronomy.44 Representativeness requires capturing the complex fluid dynamics of these specific geomorphologies, including the cyclical shifts of anabatic (upslope) and katabatic (downslope) wind flows generated by diurnal thermal forcing.25

### **Seasonal and Decadal Variation**

High-quality time-series data must seamlessly span all distinct seasonal transitions. At tropical and subtropical sites, this implies capturing the stark contrast between the wet (monsoonal or cyclonic) and dry seasons. Atmospheric turbulence and vertical PWV profiles fluctuate wildly depending on the time of year; for example, sub-millimeter atmospheric transmittance at high-altitude continental sites is heavily degraded during summer months due to rapid convective moisture transport.45

Beyond annual seasonality, the dataset must ideally capture decadal cyclical variations. Macro-oscillations such as the North Atlantic Oscillation (NAO) or the El Niño-Southern Oscillation (ENSO) fundamentally alter baseline temperatures, high-altitude wind shear, and precipitation patterns across multiple years.11 A dataset spanning only three years may capture a single, intense El Niño phase, falsely representing the baseline climatology of the site. A highly representative dataset spans a minimum of one to two decades to capture the full variance of these global oscillations.47

### **Extreme Event Coverage**

Machine learning forecasting models are notoriously brittle when tasked with predicting long-tail, low-probability extreme events. A dataset is only as robust as the variance it contains; therefore, representativeness mandates the inclusion of rare, high-impact meteorological phenomena.48 This involves capturing the passage of atmospheric rivers, severe cyclonic storms, extreme wind gusts exceeding 150 km/h, and anomalous thermal drops.49 In observatory settings, documenting extreme "dry episodes" (where PWV drops near 0.1 mm due to the sudden excursion of Antarctic air) or sudden, intense Saharan dust intrusions provides invaluable edge-case training data for anomaly detection and forecasting algorithms.33 Cleaning a dataset by removing these extreme events under the guise of "outlier removal" severely damages its predictive utility.

### **Diurnal and Observing Condition Balance**

While astronomical operations are heavily nocturnally biased, atmospheric physics is a continuous diurnal cycle. A robust dataset maintains an equal balance of daytime and nighttime observations.11 Intense solar heating of the planetary boundary layer during the day drives violent thermal convection, which subsequently decays after sunset. This decay stabilizes the atmosphere and dramatically lowers the optical seeing disk. Capturing this precise twilight transition is critical for predictive modeling.7 Advanced datasets may even employ a Solar DIMM (SDIMM) to monitor optical turbulence during daylight hours, tracking the continuous evolution of the Fried parameter.7

Furthermore, the dataset should statistically detail the distribution of weather conditions—such as the ratio of photometric (perfectly clear, stable transparency), spectroscopic (partially clear or thin cirrus), and unusable (clouded out) nights.44 This allows researchers and algorithmic schedulers to understand the base-rate probabilities of the environment.

## **4\. Benchmarking Methodology for Time-Series Forecasting**

Comparing a local weather dataset against international standards requires a rigorous mathematical and structural approach to model benchmarking. As deep learning architectures and Time Series Foundation Models (TSFMs) become the standard for weather forecasting, the methodology used to assess predictability across disparate datasets must explicitly prevent data leakage, normalize variance, and utilize standardized baseline metrics.52

### **Normalization Strategies for Cross-Dataset Comparison**

To fairly evaluate algorithmic performance across distinct datasets—which may possess vastly different scales, dynamic ranges, and base volatilities—normalization is absolutely critical. Comparing raw mean error values between a high-variance dataset (e.g., mountaintop wind speed) and a low-variance dataset (e.g., equatorial sea-level pressure) is fundamentally flawed.

Prior to model ingestion, datasets are typically subjected to z-score standardization (zero mean, unit variance) or Min-Max scaling.54 However, traditional normalization struggles with non-stationary data that exhibits shifting climatic trends over decades. Advanced forecasting models handle normalization internally through techniques like Reversible Instance Normalization (RevIN). This method normalizes each specific input time-window individually and then denormalizes the output forecast, maintaining scale invariance regardless of macroeconomic or macro-climatic shifts.

Quantitative benchmarking relies on scale-independent metrics. While Standard Mean Absolute Error (MAE) and Mean Squared Error (MSE) are useful for intra-dataset tuning, cross-dataset benchmarking requires percentage or scaled errors.55 The symmetric Mean Absolute Percentage Error (sMAPE) and Normalized Mean Absolute Error (NMAE) are established standards for this purpose 54:

### **Standardized Train/Validation/Test Split Protocols**

Evaluating time-series data presents unique structural challenges. Traditional random sampling (such as standard k-fold cross-validation) is strictly prohibited in time-series benchmarking. Randomly shuffling data destroys temporal dependencies and introduces catastrophic "future data leakage" into the training phase, allowing the model to memorize future states.56 Benchmarking must employ rigorous chronological splitting.

The industry standard is to partition the dataset chronologically into distinct continuous blocks (e.g., 70% train, 10% validation, 20% test).58 For the most rigorous evaluation, specific dynamic protocols must be employed:

1. **Expanding Window Split:** The training set sequentially expands over time, while the validation and test sets roll forward. This simulates a real-world operational environment where the forecasting model is periodically retrained as new historical data becomes available.59  
2. **Sliding Window with Gap Split:** To entirely eliminate autocorrelation leakage, a temporal gap (buffer zone) is inserted between the end of the training set and the beginning of the validation/test sets.57 This guarantees that the model relies purely on learned structural dynamics and thermodynamics, rather than relying on short-term naive persistence.

### **Baseline Models for Fair Comparison**

A new dataset must be benchmarked against a standardized hierarchy of predictive models to establish its inherent predictability and complexity.60 Running a diverse suite of models uncovers whether the dataset is dominated by simple linear trends or complex, non-linear chaos.62 The benchmarking hierarchy should include:

| Model Hierarchy | Representative Algorithms | Purpose in Benchmarking |
| :---- | :---- | :---- |
| **Naive/Statistical** | Persistence, ARIMA, Holt-Winters | Establishes absolute minimum baseline; evaluates simple linear trends and pure seasonality.63 |
| **Deep Learning** | LSTM, DeepAR, N-Hits | Evaluates short-term non-linear dependencies and basic probabilistic forecasting.65 |
| **Transformer-Based** | PatchTST, FEDformer, iTransformer | Evaluates long-term, multivariate dependencies, attention mechanisms, and frequency-domain enhancements.55 |
| **Foundation (TSFMs)** | Chronos, Moirai, TimesFM | Evaluates zero-shot capabilities; tests how well global meteorological pre-training transfers to the local microclimate.54 |

The Persistence (Naïve) model, which simply projects the last known value forward, is particularly crucial; if complex deep learning models cannot beat Persistence on a given dataset, it indicates that the environmental variables are highly stochastic or that the sampling rate is too low to capture the underlying physics.67

## **5\. Publishability Assessment and Dissemination**

Publishing a meteorological or astronomical dataset as a standalone "data paper" requires elevating the raw telemetry to a formally peer-reviewed scientific asset. Data papers focus not on novel scientific hypotheses, but on the rigorous methodology of data collection, processing, and curation, ensuring the dataset is highly usable and transparent for the broader scientific community.69

### **Criteria for a Publishable Data Paper**

Data journals subject submissions to rigorous technical peer review, focusing entirely on the integrity of the data rather than the novelty of the results. A dataset is deemed publishable if it meets several foundational criteria 71:

* **Scientific and Technical Soundness:** The methodology of collection, including hardware calibration protocols, sensor error propagation, and physical detection limits, must be exquisitely detailed.71  
* **QA/QC Documentation:** A thorough, reproducible description of the Quality Assurance and Quality Control (QA/QC) pipelines is mandatory. This includes the specific algorithms used for anomaly detection, the rules engine for flagging implausible values, homogenization techniques for integrating different sensor generations, and the exact mathematical logic used for gap-filling.4  
* **Uncertainty Quantification:** The paper must present the overall characteristics of the data in terms of spatial variance, temporal volatility, and calculated error limits for specific sensors.71  
* **FAIR Principles:** The data must be strictly Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, and Reusable. This requires depositing the data in a non-proprietary, machine-readable format (e.g., NetCDF, CSV, FITS) with open-access licensing (such as CC BY 4.0).74 Furthermore, the review process for premier journals requires that peer reviewers are granted fully anonymous access to the data repositories prior to publication.74

### **Target Journals and Repositories**

Several high-impact journals specialize in publishing peer-reviewed datasets:

* ***Earth System Science Data*** **(ESSD):** A premier venue for comprehensive climatological and atmospheric datasets. ESSD utilizes an interactive public discussion format for peer review and mandates strict adherence to open-access principles.74  
* ***Scientific Data*****:** Published by Springer Nature, this journal accepts broad, interdisciplinary environmental datasets. It requires a specific "Data Descriptor" format that meticulously details the dataset's construction and enforces strict data availability statements.77  
* ***Publications of the Astronomical Society of the Pacific*** **(PASP):** While a broader astronomical journal, PASP frequently publishes highly specialized papers relating to astronomical site testing, optical turbulence profiling, and seeing conditions.47

Data must be permanently housed in trusted, persistent repositories rather than volatile personal servers or institutional websites. Generalist repositories like Zenodo and Harvard Dataverse provide stable Digital Object Identifiers (DOIs) and version control.27 For extensive environmental data, government-backed repositories like the NOAA National Centers for Environmental Information (NCEI) or the British Oceanographic Data Centre (BODC) are highly respected and guarantee long-term preservation.73

### **Metadata Standards**

Data interoperability relies entirely on strict metadata standard compliance. Without standardized metadata, machine learning pipelines cannot autonomously parse or combine disparate datasets.

* **CF Conventions:** The Climate and Forecast (CF) Metadata Conventions are the definitive standard for meteorological and climatological data. Originally designed for NetCDF files, the CF conventions define precise, definitive standard names for physical variables (e.g., ensuring all temperature data is tagged as surface\_air\_temperature), specify dimensionless vertical coordinates, and establish standardized calendar time coordinates.81  
* **Dublin Core:** A broader, domain-agnostic metadata standard utilized by repositories to describe the general properties of the dataset (Title, Creator, Subject, Spatial Coverage, Temporal Coverage) to ensure high-level search discovery and cataloging.84  
* **IVOA Standards:** For parameters specifically related to astronomical site characterization (such as seeing, isoplanatic angle, or SQM), the International Virtual Observatory Alliance (IVOA) provides specialized frameworks. Standards like the Astronomical Dataset Characterization Data Model (CharDM) ensure the physical parameter space is fully compatible with broader astronomical archives and virtual observatory tools.85

### **Examples of Published Astronomical and Weather Datasets**

Reviewing existing published datasets provides a template for formatting and curation. Notable examples include:

* **The AntAWS Dataset:** A comprehensive compilation of Antarctic automatic weather station observations, published in ESSD, detailing 3-hourly, daily, and monthly resolutions across 267 stations from 1980 to 2021\.87  
* **The FriOWL Database:** An astronomical site characterization dataset published in MNRAS, detailing cloud cover, wind, and precipitable water vapor across 15 global observatory sites over a 45-year period.31  
* **WEATHER-5K:** A large-scale benchmark dataset for global station weather forecasting, encompassing 5,672 weather stations globally over a 10-year period, designed specifically for evaluating deep learning models.88  
* **SuryaBench:** A standardized dataset tailored for space weather tasks, including solar flare prediction and solar wind speed estimation, formatted specifically to bridge the gap between solar physics and machine learning.89

## **Conclusion**

The transformation of raw meteorological and astronomical telemetry into a benchmark-grade dataset requires an exacting dedication to scientific rigor. As demonstrated by the operational frameworks at sites like Paranal, Mauna Kea, and the Roque de los Muchachos Observatory, high-tier datasets isolate sensor noise, maintain WMO calibration traceability, and engineer highly specific features such as optical turbulence parameters (![][image4], ![][image5]) and sky luminance (SQM).

To prove utility within the modern machine learning ecosystem, the dataset must be evaluated using robust time-series benchmarking methodologies. Applying sequential temporal splits, utilizing scale-invariant metrics like sMAPE and NMAE, and benchmarking against both classical baselines and advanced Time Series Foundation Models (TSFMs) establishes the dataset's predictability and structural complexity. Finally, adhering to the CF and IVOA metadata conventions and fulfilling the stringent peer-review requirements of data journals guarantees that the dataset moves beyond a localized operational tool to become a permanent, globally interoperable scientific asset.
